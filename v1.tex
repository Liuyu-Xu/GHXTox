\begin{abstract}
Peptide toxicity is a major safety concern in peptide-based drug development,
motivating accurate computational screening methods. Existing methods have
begun to explore multi-dimensional (multimodal) feature fusion and toxicity
prediction based on the three-dimensional (3D) conformations of peptides,
yet most of them still underutilize 3D structural information, and
systematic multi-dimensional fusion remains rare. Here, we propose GHXTox, a
hierarchical dual-level fusion framework in which a 3D structural expert
first integrates sequence semantics with spatial structural features through
predicted local-distance difference test (pLDDT)-aware cross-attention, and
is then fused at the probability level with a task-adaptive one-dimensional
(1D) sequence expert using weights frozen on training out-of-fold (OOF)
predictions. The 1D branch analyzes sequences with a protein language model
(ProtT5 fine-tuned with low-rank adaptation, LoRA) and a bidirectional Mamba
state-space model. The 3D expert integrates an ESMFold-derived structural
anchor, pLDDT-aware residue geometry, and full-atom-derived chemical sites.
Multi-conformer information is retained only in training-side representation
learning and controlled ablations; benchmark and public inference always use
the single-structure base logits. On the frozen historical benchmark test sets,
GHXTox achieves MCC values of 0.8705 and 0.6673 and AUPRC values of 0.9674 and
0.8046 on Test1 and Test2, respectively. Further analyses characterize
residue-level sequence sensitivity, confidence--gate associations,
chemical-site perturbations, expert disagreement, and an OOF-frozen
selective-prediction rule. At realized coverages of 88.54\% and 86.94\%, the
selective rule reduced the accepted-sample error to 2.71\% and 3.16\% on
Test1 and Test2 without benchmark-specific cutoff selection. These findings
are interpreted as model-level evidence rather than causal biological mechanisms.
Frozen-model analyses indicate that the conformer contribution is small and
non-uniform: under the structural expert, substantial geometric
variation does not necessarily translate into stable outcome-aligned
probability variation.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} peptide toxicity prediction; multimodal deep learning; protein language model;
bidirectional Mamba; pLDDT-aware multi-scale 3D modeling; interpretability

\section{INTRODUCTION}

Peptides are increasingly used as therapeutic candidates because of their
high target specificity and functional diversity
\cite{muttenthaler2021}. However, peptide toxicity remains a major safety
concern during drug development, and the accurate and efficient
identification of toxic peptides is a major bottleneck in peptide-based drug
discovery \cite{zhang2025toxmsrc}. Because conventional experimental
toxicity assays are costly, time-consuming, and difficult to scale, reliable
computational predictors are highly desirable.

Computational peptide toxicity prediction has evolved from traditional machine
learning methods based on handcrafted physicochemical features toward deep
learning approaches with stronger representation-learning capability. Current
mainstream methods can be roughly grouped into two categories. The first
category operates on sequence-level (one-dimensional, 1D) and/or atomic-level
(two-dimensional, 2D) representations. For example, ToxMSRC combines
continuous bag-of-words (CBOW) embeddings, multi-scale convolutional neural
networks, and a bidirectional long short-term memory network with residual
connections, achieving strong performance on the benchmark dataset \cite{zhang2025toxmsrc}.
ToxPLTC integrates pooled ProtT5 embeddings, borderline-SMOTE augmentation,
and a text convolutional neural network \cite{liang2026toxpltc}. ToxiPep fuses context-aware
sequence representations with atomic-level graphs through a cross-attention
mechanism \cite{guan2025toxipep}. HyPepTox-Fuse further combines embeddings from multiple protein
language models with conventional descriptors \cite{tran2025hypeptoxfuse}. Although these models
achieve strong performance within their respective modalities, they largely
neglect the three-dimensional (3D) conformations of peptides, which are
closely related to toxicity; cross-dimensional information interaction and
mutual validation remain limited, and their interpretability is mostly
confined to the sequence level.

The second category attempts to exploit 3D structural information. PeptiTox
uses ESM2 sequence embeddings together with ESMFold-predicted 3D structures
and geometric graph neural networks \cite{wang2025peptitox}. StrucToxNet feeds ProtT5 sequence
embeddings and ESMFold-predicted structures into an equivariant graph neural
network (EGNN) and systematically analyzes structural quality \cite{jiao2025structoxnet}. These
studies demonstrate the value of 3D conformations for toxicity prediction.
However, such methods are constrained by the limited accuracy of current
structure prediction models on short peptides and typically rely on a single
predicted conformation; structural confidence, i.e., the predicted local
distance difference test (pLDDT) score, is either discarded through hard
filtering or ignored, and multi-conformer ensembles are not considered.
Moreover, the fusion between the sequence and structure branches remains
relatively shallow, and no systematic multi-dimensional fusion that combines a
task-adaptive sequence model with confidence-aware multi-scale structure
modeling has been established.

To address these limitations, we propose GHXTox, an interpretable hierarchical
dual-level fusion framework for peptide toxicity prediction. A task-adaptive
1D sequence expert combines a ProtT5 protein language model, adapted by
low-rank adaptation (LoRA), with a bidirectional Mamba state-space encoder
and three-seed ensembling \cite{elnaggar2022prottrans,hu2022lora,gu2024mamba}.
A sequence-enhanced 3D
structural expert integrates sequence semantics with spatial structure
through pLDDT-aware cross-attention, ESMFold-derived geometry, full-atom
chemical sites, and confidence gating across multiple structural scales;
PepFlow and AlphaFold2/ColabFold conformer ensembles informed only
training-side analyses and controlled ablations, and the final predictor uses
the single-structure ESMFold anchor
\cite{lin2023esmfold,jumper2021alphafold2,mirdita2022colabfold,abdin2024pepflow}.
The two expert probabilities are fused at the probability level with weights
fixed on training out-of-fold (OOF) predictions; the 1D probabilities are
first calibrated on the same OOF table, and no benchmark labels are used
during this selection. Gated sequence--structure
cross-attention inside the structural expert further informs the interaction
design \cite{zhang2026neuroppredpscg}. Ablation experiments show that the
contribution of structural evidence is dataset- and metric-dependent: fusion
improves several fixed-threshold and probability-quality measures on Test1
while maintaining broadly comparable performance on Test2. Post hoc analyses
characterize residue-level sensitivity, confidence gating, chemical-site
perturbations, and expert disagreement without assigning causal biological
meaning to these signals. The framework is evaluated on the frozen historical
benchmarks described in Section 2.1.

In summary, the main contributions of this work are threefold: (1) we propose
GHXTox, an interpretable hierarchical dual-level fusion framework that
explores the complementary joint use of one-dimensional sequence and
three-dimensional conformational information, integrating a task-adaptive 1D
protein language model with pLDDT-aware multi-scale 3D structure modeling and
combining the two experts at the probability level (Sections 2.5 and 3.2); (2) the 3D branch designs a multi-scale structural
information extraction strategy spanning from local residue functional groups
to the whole peptide, and incorporates pLDDT confidence into structural
feature processing through continuous down-weighting and dynamic gating; and
(3) extensive experiments on the frozen historical benchmark test sets show
that GHXTox improves several fixed-threshold and probability-quality measures
on Test1 while maintaining broadly comparable performance on Test2, while
ablation, robustness, and interpretability analyses characterize the
conditions under which sequence and structural evidence are complementary.

\subsection{Datasets}
We evaluate GHXTox on the benchmark datasets constructed by CAPTP \cite{jiao2024captp} and
subsequently adopted by ToxMSRC \cite{zhang2025toxmsrc}. Following the data construction protocol
of ToxMSRC \cite{zhang2025toxmsrc}, toxic peptide sequences were merged from three databases,
namely CSM-Toxin \cite{morozov2023csmboxin}, ToxinPred2 \cite{sharma2022toxinpred2}, and ATSE \cite{wei2021atse}, and sequences longer
than 50 residues were removed. Non-toxic sequences were retrieved from UniProt
\cite{uniprot2019} using the query ``NOT KW-0800 AND NOT KW-0020 AND reviewed:true'', and
sequences containing non-standard amino acids or exceeding 50 residues were
excluded. After combining the two groups and removing samples with conflicting
labels, CD-HIT \cite{fu2012cdhit} was applied with a sequence-identity threshold of 0.9 to
eliminate redundant sequences, yielding 2{,}138 toxic and 5{,}375 non-toxic
peptides. Following the same partition as ToxMSRC \cite{zhang2025toxmsrc}, we used 85\% of the data
as the training set and the remaining 15\% as the benchmark Test1 set,
resulting in 1{,}818 toxic and
4{,}569 non-toxic training peptides and 320 toxic and 806 non-toxic Test1
peptides. An additional benchmark Test2 set, containing 46 toxic and 536
non-toxic peptides, is used for frozen historical evaluation. Both Test1 and
Test2 had been observed during historical project development and are
therefore treated as frozen historical benchmarks rather than newly blinded
external validation sets; their labels were not used in the formal OOF
calibration, fusion-weight selection, or threshold-freezing procedure.
ToxinPred~3.0 \cite{rathore2024toxinpred3} was consulted as an external method
and data-source reference during project development, but its records were not
added to the present training set or to the formal Test1/Test2 result tables.
Table~\ref{tab:datasets} summarizes the statistics of the datasets used in
this study.

\subsection{Overview of the Model}
GHXTox is a hierarchical dual-level fusion framework for peptide toxicity
prediction: a task-adaptive one-dimensional (1D) sequence expert and a
sequence-enhanced three-dimensional (3D) structural expert generate
independent probabilities, interact internally at the feature level, and are
combined at the probability level.

The 1D expert processes the peptide sequence with a LoRA-adapted ProtT5
encoder \cite{elnaggar2022prottrans,hu2022lora} followed by a bidirectional
Mamba state-space model \cite{gu2024mamba}, averages three seed models, and
applies OOF-fitted Platt calibration to produce $p_{\mathrm{1D}}$
(Section 2.3). The 3D expert integrates sequence semantics, residue
geometry, full-atom chemical sites, and pLDDT confidence at five scales,
combines sequence and spatial states through pLDDT-aware cross-attention,
and emits $p_{\mathrm{SE3D}}$ (Section 2.4).

The two probabilities are combined at the probability level; the fusion
rule, the decision threshold, and the final output are described in
Section 2.5.

\subsection{Task-Adaptive 1D Sequence Modeling}
The 1D sequence expert learns toxicity-related contextual semantics directly
from peptide sequences. Instead of reducing a frozen protein-language-model
output to a fixed mean vector, GHXTox preserves the residue-level
representations of ProtT5, adapts the pretrained encoder to the toxicity task
through low-rank adaptation (LoRA), models residue dependencies with a
bidirectional Mamba encoder, and aggregates the residue states by masked mean
pooling before classification. Three full-data models with different random
seeds are combined by equal-weight probability averaging followed by Platt
calibration fitted exclusively on training out-of-fold (OOF) predictions.

Each residue is first written in the space-separated format expected by the
ProtT5 tokenizer, and a binary residue-validity mask separates genuine residue
positions from special and padding tokens; this mask is reused throughout the
Mamba scans and in the final pooling. The tokenized sequence is processed by
the ProtT5-XL-UniRef50 encoder \cite{elnaggar2022prottrans} with LoRA, yielding
residue-level representations that retain position-specific contextual
semantics. To adapt this general-purpose encoder to the toxicity task, LoRA
\cite{hu2022lora} is applied to all of its linear layers with rank $r=8$,
scaling $\alpha=16$, and dropout 0.1: the pretrained weights stay frozen, and
only the zero-initialized low-rank update ($\mathbf W = \mathbf W_0 +
\frac{\alpha}{r}\mathbf B\mathbf A$) and the downstream modules are trainable.
The 1024-dimensional representations are then projected into the
192-dimensional Mamba hidden space through a layer-norm--GELU--dropout block.

Two bidirectional Mamba layers \cite{gu2024mamba} are then stacked after the
projection block to capture the sequential dependencies relevant to peptide
toxicity. Each directional block follows the standard selective state-space
formulation with input-dependent discretization and a 16-dimensional recurrent
state; forward and backward scans are applied to the masked sequence, and their
outputs are concatenated, fused by a linear layer, and added through a residual
connection, so every residue is contextualized by both its N-terminal and
C-terminal contexts. Padding positions are zeroed so that they cannot inject
information into the recurrent states. The resulting residue states are
aggregated by masked mean pooling over the valid positions, and a
layer-normalized single-output linear head yields the uncalibrated toxicity
probability. Because non-toxic peptides far outnumber toxic peptides in the
training data, a positive-class-weighted binary cross-entropy loss is used
instead of SMOTE or Borderline-SMOTE, with the positive-class weight
$w_+ = N_-/N_+$ recomputed from each fold's training partition; this emphasizes
the minority class without generating synthetic samples or leaking held-out
information.

To reduce the variance of a single training run, three 1D models are trained
under identical settings with random seeds 42, 123, and 2025, and the ensemble
score is the equal-weight arithmetic average of the three full-data models; no
seed is selected on test performance and no seed-specific weight is introduced.
Although averaging reduces variance, neural-network probabilities may remain
miscalibrated, so a monotonic two-parameter Platt calibrator \cite{platt1999}
is fitted to the training OOF scores (generated with the fixed group-aware
five-fold partition, Section 2.5) and applied to the three-seed average at
inference. Because the calibration is monotonic, it preserves the sample
ranking. Test1/Test2 labels are not used for member selection, calibration
fitting, or threshold optimization. The calibrated probability $p_{\mathrm{1D}}$
is the sequence-expert output used in the top-level fusion (Section 2.5); no
additional calibrator is fitted after fusion.

The complete 1D pipeline, from the peptide sequence to the calibrated
probability, is summarized as follows:
\begin{align}
    \mathbf H^{\mathrm{PLM}} &= \operatorname{ProtT5}_{\mathrm{LoRA}}(\mathcal{S})
        \in \mathbb{R}^{L\times1024},\quad
    \mathbf X^{(0)} = \operatorname{Proj}\left[\mathbf H^{\mathrm{PLM}}\right]
        \in \mathbb{R}^{L\times192},\label{eq:1d_encode}\\
    \mathbf h_{1D} &= \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{L} m_i\,
        \operatorname{BiMamba}\left(\mathbf X^{(0)}\right)_i}
        {\sum_{i=1}^{L} m_i} \in \mathbb{R}^{192},\label{eq:1d_pool}\\
    z_{1D} &= \mathbf w_c^{\top}\operatorname{LN}\left(\mathbf h_{1D}\right) + b_c,\qquad
    p_{1D}^{\mathrm{raw}} = \sigma\left(z_{1D}\right),\label{eq:1d_head}\\
    p_{1D} &= \sigma\left(a\,\operatorname{logit}\left(\operatorname{clip}\left(
        \frac{1}{3}\sum_{k=1}^{3} p^{(k)},\;\varepsilon,\;1-\varepsilon\right)\right)+b\right)
        \label{eq:1d_calib}
\end{align}
where $\operatorname{Proj}$ is the layer-norm--GELU--dropout projection,
$\mathbf m$ is the residue-validity mask, $p^{(k)}$ is the probability of the
$k$-th seed model, and $a,b$ are the OOF-fitted monotonic Platt parameters
(with logit clip $\varepsilon = 10^{-6}$).

\subsection{pLDDT-Aware Multi-Scale 3D Structural Modeling}
Peptide toxicity is influenced not only by amino acid composition and linear
residue order but also by the three-dimensional (3D) arrangement of charged
groups, hydrophobic regions, aromatic systems, and sulfur-containing
moieties. Because peptides are generally short, conformationally flexible,
and poorly covered by experimentally determined structures, treating a single
predicted conformation as an error-free structural observation may amplify
structure-prediction errors, whereas C$\alpha$-only or peptide-level summary
representations fail to capture the local spatial-chemical environments that
are relevant to toxicity.

To address these limitations, GHXTox employs a pLDDT-aware multi-scale
structural expert that captures complementary information at five levels:
residue semantics, spatial residue neighborhoods, full-atom chemical sites,
peptide-level global properties, and conformer-ensemble statistics
(Figure~\ref{fig:expert_detail}). Sections 2.4.1--2.4.6 describe, in turn,
single-structure prediction and conformer preparation, residue feature
construction and the hybrid geometric graph, pLDDT-aware equivariant graph
encoding, full-atom chemical-site message passing with the
availability-masked conformer-ensemble logit residual, sequence--structure
cross-attention, and global feature integration with classification. Throughout, pLDDT is treated as a
continuous reliability signal rather than a hard filter: low-confidence
structural evidence is down-weighted in geometric edge weighting,
chemical-site messages, and residue- and peptide-level structural gating
instead of being removed, preserving structural coverage while limiting the
influence of uncertain coordinates. pLDDT is interpreted here as a local
confidence estimate produced by the structure-prediction model, not as an
experimentally determined measure of structural accuracy, conformational
flexibility, or biological activity.

\subsubsection{Peptide Structure Prediction and Conformer Preparation}
For a peptide sequence of length $L$, $S = (a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_L)$,
ESMFold is first used to predict its three-dimensional structure
\cite{lin2023esmfold}. The predicted structure is stored as a full-atom PDB file,
from which residue-level C$\alpha$ coordinates, backbone coordinates,
full-atom-derived chemical-site coordinates, and pLDDT scores are extracted.
The C$\alpha$ coordinates are collected as $\mathbf X = [\mathbf x_1, \ldots,
\mathbf x_L]^{\top} \in \mathbb{R}^{L\times3}$, $\mathcal{A}$ denotes the
full-atom-PDB-derived backbone and chemical-site geometry, and
$\mathbf q = (q_1, \ldots, q_L) \in [0,1]^L$ contains the residue-level pLDDT
confidence scores rescaled to $[0,1]$, where $q_i$ denotes the residue-level
pLDDT score of residue $i$. The C$\alpha$ coordinates serve as the primary spatial variables of
the residue-level equivariant graph, whereas the full-atom coordinates are
used to construct local chemical sites. Before geometric encoding, the
coordinates are centered at the peptide centroid to remove the effect of
absolute translation.

A single predicted structure provides a common structural anchor but cannot
represent potential conformational heterogeneity. The conformer-residual
stage was therefore trained on a multi-source conformer variant using a
2{,}795-peptide cohort with up to eight validated, aligned conformers per
peptide. PepFlow is a generalized Boltzmann generator that performs direct
all-atom conformational-ensemble sampling from the peptide energy landscape
rather than predicting a single structure; it samples eight conformations per
input peptide during training and has been shown to efficiently recapitulate
experimental ensembles of short linear motifs (SLiMs, typically 3--15
residues) and to generate all-atom conformations for peptides of different
lengths \cite{abdin2024pepflow}. Short-peptide conformations are therefore
provided by PepFlow, which is applied to peptides of at most 15 residues (the
actual training cohort spans 11--15 residues and comprises 761 records with
eight conformers each). Longer peptides are provided by AlphaFold2/ColabFold:
AlphaFold2 is a high-accuracy single-structure predictor
\cite{jumper2021alphafold2}, and its peptide-structure performance has been
systematically evaluated for peptides of 16--60 residues
\cite{gulsevin2022benchmarking}, an interval that covers the 16--30-residue
cohort; ColabFold, an MSA-accelerated AlphaFold2 implementation
\cite{mirdita2022colabfold}, generates candidate conformations from five
models and multiple seeds, which are filtered by an average-pLDDT quality
floor and then reduced to eight diverse conformers per peptide by
farthest-point sampling on Kabsch-aligned C$\alpha$-RMSD, yielding 2{,}034
training records. These sources are represented explicitly in the residual
head; nevertheless, generator identity remains partially coupled to peptide
length and is treated as a limitation rather than as an independently
identified causal factor.

For controlled ablation only, a test-side conformer cache was constructed
with fixed source and sequence-matching policies without using Test1 or Test2
labels; these caches are not used by the final benchmark predictor or the
public inference interface.
To remove arbitrary rigid-body differences and make the statistics comparable
across peptides, every admitted ensemble is normalized before being consumed
by the residual branch. An ensemble is represented as
$\mathcal{C} = \{\mathbf X^{(1)}, \ldots, \mathbf X^{(M)}\}$, $1 \leq M \leq
8$, where $\mathbf X^{(m)} \in \mathbb{R}^{L\times3}$ denotes the C$\alpha$
coordinates of conformer $m$. First, each conformer must reproduce the input
FASTA sequence residue by residue (both the C$\alpha$ sequence and the
chemical-site parse are verified); non-matching conformers are rejected, so
all conformers share the same residue-index order without reordering or
truncation. Second, taking the first conformer as the reference, every
conformer is centered and aligned to the reference frame by Kabsch alignment,
which obtains an optimal rotation by singular value decomposition with
reflection correction, yielding the aligned coordinates and the
per-residue fluctuation:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \tilde{\mathbf X}^{(m)} &= \left(\mathbf X^{(m)} - \mathbf 1_L
        \bar{\mathbf x}^{(m)\top}\right)\mathbf R_m,
        \qquad \mathbf R_m = \mathbf U_m \mathbf V_m^{\top},\\
        \mathrm{RMSF}_i &= \left[\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}
        \left\lVert \tilde{\mathbf x}_i^{(m)} - \bar{\mathbf x}_i
        \right\rVert_2^2\right]^{1/2},
    \end{split}
    \label{eq:conformer_align}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{\mathbf X}^{(m)}$ denotes the aligned C$\alpha$ coordinates of
conformer $m$, $\mathbf 1_L$ is the all-ones vector, $\bar{\mathbf x}^{(m)}$
is the centroid of conformer $m$, $\mathbf R_m$ is the optimal rotation
obtained from the singular value decomposition of the centered
cross-covariance between conformer $m$ and the reference conformer (with
reflection correction), $\bar{\mathbf x}_i$ is the ensemble-mean coordinate of
residue $i$, and $\mathrm{RMSF}_i$ is the corresponding per-residue
root-mean-square fluctuation.

After alignment, the ensemble-mean coordinates,
per-residue root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), and per-conformer RMSD are
computed, whereas the rotation/translation-invariant quantities -- per-conformer
radius of gyration, per-residue-pair distance mean and standard deviation, and
6/8/10 Å contact occupancies (the fraction of conformers in which a residue
pair is closer than the cutoff) -- are computed directly from the raw
coordinates. The resulting statistics are rescaled by fixed factors (e.g.,
RMSF/10 and distances/20) into bounded residue-level (8-dimensional) and
peptide-level (12-dimensional) features, and the conformer count is normalized
as $M/8$. Regarding confidence, AlphaFold2/ColabFold conformers carry the
real pLDDT read from the PDB B-factor column, whereas PepFlow does not output
pLDDT and is assigned a fixed substitute value of 0.7; this substitute is
explicitly not treated as pLDDT in the single-structure quality gating, which
remains governed by the ESMFold backbone, and PepFlow contributes only
cross-conformer fluctuation and occupancy information. The normalized
ensemble statistics are consumed by a small logit residual branch controlled
by a binary availability indicator $c_{\mathrm{conf}}$ (mechanism detailed in
Section 2.4.4); when $c_{\mathrm{conf}} = 0$, the correction vanishes exactly
and the model reduces to the single-structure pathway. Missing conformer
statistics are treated as unavailable data rather than as evidence of
structural rigidity; the model draws no flexibility inference from the absence
of conformers and simply falls back to the single-structure prediction.


The generated conformers are computational samples rather than experimentally
determined trajectories or rigorous thermodynamic ensembles. Consequently,
RMSF, contact occupancy, and distance variance are interpreted as
sampling-derived descriptors of conformational variation rather than direct
measurements of peptide dynamics in solution.

\subsubsection{Residue Feature Construction and Hybrid Geometric Graph}
Given the predicted single structure and its confidence scores (Section 2.4.1),
the structural expert constructs two complementary residue-level
representations: an internal sequence-semantic representation and a
spatial-geometric representation. The sequence-semantic subbranch uses frozen
ESM2 residue embeddings \cite{lin2023esmfold} and a lightweight Transformer;
unlike the independent top-level 1D prediction expert of Section 2.3, it
serves the residue-level sequence--structure alignment inside the structural
expert.

For residue $i$, the input to the internal sequence-semantic subbranch is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf u_i^{\mathrm{seq}} = \left[\mathbf e_i^{\mathrm{AA}} \parallel
    \mathbf e_i^{\mathrm{group}} \parallel \mathbf f_i^{\mathrm{res}} \parallel
    P_{\mathrm{ESM}}\left(\mathbf e_i^{\mathrm{ESM2}}\right)\right],
    \label{eq:seq_feat}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf e_i^{\mathrm{AA}} \in \mathbb{R}^{32}$ is the amino acid
category embedding, $\mathbf e_i^{\mathrm{group}} \in \mathbb{R}^{12}$ is the
functional-group category embedding, $\mathbf f_i^{\mathrm{res}} \in
\mathbb{R}^{42}$ denotes the explicit residue-level physicochemical
descriptors, and $\mathbf e_i^{\mathrm{ESM2}} \in \mathbb{R}^{1280}$ is the
ESM2-650M residue representation; the projection $P_{\mathrm{ESM}}$ maps the
ESM2 representation to 128 dimensions. Stacking the residue-level feature
vectors yields the sequence-feature matrix $U_{\mathrm{seq}} \in
\mathbb{R}^{L \times 214}$ ($214 = 32 + 12 + 42 + 128$). After a linear
projection and positional encoding, a two-layer Transformer encoder (hidden
size 128, four attention heads, feed-forward dimension 512) produces the
contextualized sequence states $\mathbf H^{\mathrm{seq}} \in
\mathbb{R}^{L \times 128}$, which encode the contextual sequence semantics of
each residue and serve as the query representation in the
sequence--structure cross-attention module.

In the spatial subbranch, the initial representation of residue $i$ is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf h_i^{(0)} = P_{\mathrm{sp}}\left[\mathbf f_i^{\mathrm{res}}
    \parallel \mathbf g_i^{\mathrm{geo}} \parallel q_i \parallel
    P_{\mathrm{ESM}}\left(\mathbf e_i^{\mathrm{ESM2}}\right)\right],
    \label{eq:residue_init}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf g_i^{\mathrm{geo}} \in \mathbb{R}^{16}$ is a lightweight
geometric descriptor derived from the C$\alpha$ coordinates and pLDDT scores,
including (i) residue-level pLDDT and local confidence statistics;
(ii) radial position, contact fractions, and soft spatial density;
(iii) nearest-neighbor, nonlocal-neighbor, and local sequential distances;
and (iv) sine/cosine encodings of the C$\alpha$-trace angles and torsions.
The residue-level confidence $q_i$ additionally enters the initial
representation as an explicit scalar. These deterministic descriptors
provide stable local geometric priors without imposing an overly complex
handcrafted representation for short and uncertain predicted peptide
structures.

GHXTox then constructs a hybrid residue graph
$\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{seq}} \cup
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{knn}})$ in which each residue corresponds to one node:
sequential edges $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{seq}}$ connect adjacent residues and
preserve peptide-chain continuity, whereas $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{knn}}$
connects each residue to its 10 nearest spatial neighbors and is symmetrized.
The hybrid graph therefore captures both covalent sequence order and nonlocal
spatial contacts while avoiding large variations in graph density that may
arise from a single fixed-distance cutoff; equivalently,
$A_{ij} \in \{0,1\}$ indicates whether residues $i$ and $j$ are connected by
a sequential or k-nearest-neighbor edge.

For residues $i$ and $j$, the C$\alpha$ distance
$d_{ij} = \lVert \mathbf x_i - \mathbf x_j \rVert_2$ is encoded by 16 radial
basis functions with centers $\mu_k$ uniformly spaced over 0--24 Å,
$\phi_k(d_{ij}) = \exp[-\gamma(d_{ij}-\mu_k)^2]$ with
$\gamma = 1/(\mu_{k+1}-\mu_k)^2$. The base edge representation additionally
includes the pairwise structural confidence and the normalized sequence
separation:
\begin{equation}
    c_{ij} = \sqrt{q_i q_j}, \qquad
    s_{ij} = \frac{\log(1 + |i - j|)}{\log(L + 1)},
    \label{eq:edge_feat}
\end{equation}
so that $s_{ij}$ is small for sequentially adjacent residues and approaches
one for distant residues. The resulting edge feature is therefore
$\mathbf e_{ij} = \left[\boldsymbol\phi(d_{ij}) \parallel
c_{ij} \parallel s_{ij}\right]$, which lets the model distinguish
sequentially adjacent contacts, long-range spatial contacts, and contacts
supported by different levels of structural confidence.
\subsubsection{pLDDT-Aware Equivariant Graph Encoding}
To learn three-dimensional representations without relying on a particular
global coordinate frame, the spatial branch employs three E(3)-equivariant
graph neural network layers \cite{satorras2021egnn}. The network updates
residue features using rotation- and translation-invariant quantities while
updating coordinates equivariantly. Consequently, a global rigid
transformation of the peptide structure does not change the predicted
toxicity, whereas intermediate coordinate representations transform
consistently with the input.

At layer $\ell$, the message from residue $j$ to residue $i$ is computed as
$\mathbf m_{ij}^{(\ell)} = \phi_e^{(\ell)}[\mathbf h_i^{(\ell)} \parallel
\mathbf h_j^{(\ell)} \parallel \mathbf e_{ij}^{(\ell)}]$, where
$\phi_e^{(\ell)}$ is a message multilayer perceptron. To prevent uncertain or
distant contacts from contributing as strongly as local high-confidence
contacts, a continuous edge weight is defined as
\begin{equation}
    w_{ij}^{(\ell)} = A_{ij}\, \exp\!\left(-\frac{d_{ij}^{(\ell)}}{12}\right)
    \left[q_{\min} + \left(1 - q_{\min}\right) c_{ij}^{\rho}\right],
    \qquad q_{\min} = 0.1, \quad \rho = 1.5,
    \label{eq:edge_weight}
\end{equation}
The term $\exp(-d_{ij}^{(\ell)}/12)$ imposes a smooth distance decay with
a characteristic decay length of 12 Å, and both the distance and the edge
weight are recomputed at each layer from the current coordinates. Here,
$A_{ij} \in \{0,1\}$ is the adjacency indicator of the hybrid graph defined
in Section 2.4.2, and $c_{ij} = \sqrt{q_i q_j}$ is the pairwise structural
confidence derived from the residue-level pLDDT scores $q_i$ and $q_j$
defined in Section 2.4.1. The minimum weight $q_{\min}$ prevents
low-confidence regions from being completely disconnected, whereas the
exponent $\rho$ increases the relative contribution of high-confidence
contacts. The distance-decay term further reduces the influence of more
distant spatial neighbors.

These pLDDT-aware weighted edge messages are then aggregated at each node
with a normalization floor. For node $i$, the aggregated message is
$\mathbf m_i^{(\ell)} = \sum_j w_{ij}^{(\ell)}\mathbf m_{ij}^{(\ell)} /
\max(\sum_j w_{ij}^{(\ell)}, 1)$, the node features are updated as
$\mathbf h_i^{(\ell+1)} = \operatorname{LN}\{\mathbf h_i^{(\ell)} +
\phi_h^{(\ell)}[\mathbf h_i^{(\ell)} \parallel \mathbf m_i^{(\ell)}]\}$, and
the coordinates are updated equivariantly as $\mathbf x_i^{(\ell+1)} =
\mathbf x_i^{(\ell)} + 0.05 \sum_j \alpha_{ij}^{(\ell)}(\mathbf
x_i^{(\ell)} - \mathbf x_j^{(\ell)}) / \max(\sum_j w_{ij}^{(\ell)}, 1)$,
where $\alpha_{ij}^{(\ell)} = \tanh[\phi_x^{(\ell)}(\mathbf
m_{ij}^{(\ell)})]\, w_{ij}^{(\ell)}$ is a bounded coordinate-update
coefficient generated from the edge message and pairwise distances. The
coordinates are recentered after every layer to reduce numerical drift.
Because the update depends only on scalar messages, distances, and relative
coordinate vectors, the operation remains equivariant to rigid rotations and
translations.

Each layer update performs one round of message passing, i.e., one hop of
spatial-context propagation over the hybrid graph, so a single layer can
aggregate only the direct neighborhood of each node. Because toxicity-related
spatial patterns often span multiple residues (for example, spatial clusters
formed by several charged, aromatic, or hydrophobic residues), the model must
aggregate multi-hop neighborhood information along the graph; stacking layers
therefore enlarges the receptive field hop by hop: the first layer primarily
encodes direct geometric neighbors, whereas subsequent layers integrate
higher-order spatial environments through repeated message passing. GHXTox
uses three layers, a depth that balances context integration against model
complexity and overfitting risk on short and uncertain predicted peptide
structures. Unlike approaches that simply append pLDDT as an ordinary node
feature, GHXTox uses pLDDT to directly modulate the strength of geometric
messages, allowing structural uncertainty to influence representation
learning at the propagation stage. Low pLDDT does not imply that a residue is
biologically unimportant; it may reflect prediction error or genuine
conformational flexibility, so the model reduces the reliability of uncertain
coordinate evidence while preserving the residue's sequence-semantic and
physicochemical features.
\subsubsection{Full-Atom Chemical-Site Message Passing}
A C$\alpha$-based residue graph captures the global fold and spatial
neighborhoods but cannot resolve chemically distinct moieties within the same
side chain. For example, tyrosine contains both an aromatic ring and a
hydroxyl group, whereas tryptophan contains an aromatic system and an indole
nitrogen. Compressing each side chain into a single centroid would mix these
chemically different moieties. GHXTox therefore extracts chemistry-aware
pseudo-sites from the predicted full-atom PDB structure and processes them
with a dedicated local chemical branch.

To retain this chemical detail, up to two chemical sites are kept for each
residue. Site identity is represented by an eight-dimensional multi-hot
vector $\mathbf t_{ir} \in \{0,1\}^{8}$ allowing overlapping types: positive,
negative, donor, acceptor, aromatic, hydrophobic, sulfur, and conformational,
where $r$ indexes the chemical site within residue $i$. Multiple types may be
assigned to the same site: for example, the cysteine sulfur site is labeled
as donor, hydrophobic, and sulfur-containing, whereas the proline ring is
labeled as hydrophobic and conformationally constrained; the aromatic ring
and hydroxyl group of tyrosine are represented as separate sites, as are the
aromatic system and the indole NE1 nitrogen (donor) of tryptophan.

Each site is further localized and oriented in the predicted structure: its
coordinate is the mean position of the heavy atoms defining the corresponding
chemical moiety, and its orientation is represented by either a bond
direction or an unsigned plane normal. Because the sign of a plane normal is
arbitrary, downstream directional features use absolute cosines, preserving
rotation invariance and eliminating orientation-sign ambiguity. Combining
these attributes, the initial representation of site $r$ in residue $i$ is
$\mathbf z_{ir}^{(0)} = \phi_{\mathrm{site}}[\mathbf t_{ir} \parallel q_i
\parallel o_{ir}^{\mathrm{valid}}]$, where $q_i$ is the pLDDT score of the
parent residue and $o_{ir}^{\mathrm{valid}}$ indicates whether a reliable
orientation could be constructed; the input is projected into a 64-dimensional
hidden space.

Because chemical interactions depend on spatial proximity, edges are
established between valid sites separated by no more than 16~Å; same-residue
site edges are kept, preserving spatial relationships between chemically
different moieties in complex side chains. Each edge carries four
complementary feature families. First, the raw distance in Å is encoded using
16 radial basis functions, $\boldsymbol\phi_{\mathrm{raw}}(d_{uv}) \in
\mathbb{R}^{16}$. Second, a peptide-size-normalized distance is constructed.
The radius of gyration of the C$\alpha$ coordinates is
\begin{equation}
    R_g = \sqrt{\frac{1}{L} \sum_{i=1}^{L}
    \left\lVert \mathbf x_i - \bar{\mathbf x} \right\rVert_2^2},
    \qquad \bar{\mathbf x} = \frac{1}{L} \sum_{i=1}^{L} \mathbf x_i,
    \qquad \bar{d}_{uv} = \frac{d_{uv}}{R_g},
    \label{eq:normalized_distance}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{\mathbf x}$ denotes the centroid of the valid C$\alpha$
coordinates, and the normalized distance is encoded by eight radial basis
functions over the interval 0--4, $\boldsymbol\phi_{\mathrm{norm}}(\bar{d}_{uv})
\in \mathbb{R}^{8}$. The raw representation describes the physical separation
in Å, whereas the normalized representation describes the separation relative
to the overall peptide size, allowing the model to distinguish, for example,
an 8~Å contact in a compact short peptide from an 8~Å contact in a larger
peptide. Third, four rotation-invariant orientation features are used,
$\mathbf o_{uv} = [|\mathbf o_u \cdot \mathbf r_{uv}|,\,
|\mathbf o_v \cdot \mathbf r_{vu}|,\, |\mathbf o_u \cdot \mathbf o_v|,\,
o_{uv}^{\mathrm{valid}}]$, which describe the alignment of the source
orientation with the intersite direction, the alignment of the target
orientation with the reverse direction, the mutual orientation of the two
sites, and orientation availability. Fourth, six explicit compatibility
channels are used: opposite-charge, donor--acceptor, aromatic--aromatic,
positive--aromatic (cation--$\pi$), sulfur--sulfur, and
hydrophobic--hydrophobic compatibility. These channels encode candidate
chemical compatibility patterns available to the model; they do not imply
that a true salt bridge, hydrogen bond, cation--$\pi$ interaction, disulfide
bond, or hydrophobic core is present in the predicted structure.

Combining these families yields the full chemical-site edge representation
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf e_{uv}^{\mathrm{chem}} = \left[\boldsymbol\phi_{\mathrm{raw}}
    (d_{uv}) \parallel \boldsymbol\phi_{\mathrm{norm}}(\bar{d}_{uv})
    \parallel \mathbf o_{uv} \parallel \mathbf c_{uv}^{\mathrm{chem}}
    \parallel \sqrt{q_u q_v}\right],
    \label{eq:site_edge}
\end{equation}
in which pLDDT enters the chemical-site features as a confidence channel. At
layer $l$, each site message is computed from the two site states and the
edge representation, weighted by the pairwise confidence, and aggregated over
the neighborhood. After two message-passing layers, valid sites belonging to
the same residue are mean-pooled, and the residue-level chemical residual and
the chemical-enhanced spatial state are
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathbf m_{uv}^{(l)} &= \phi_{\mathrm{chem}}^{(l)}
        \left[\mathbf z_u^{(l)} \parallel \mathbf z_v^{(l)}
        \parallel \mathbf e_{uv}^{\mathrm{chem}}\right],\\
        \bar{\mathbf m}_v^{(l)} &= \frac{\sum_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)}
        \sqrt{q_u q_v}\, \mathbf m_{uv}^{(l)}}
        {\max\!\left(\sum_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)} \sqrt{q_u q_v},\, 1\right)},\\
        \Delta \mathbf h_i^{\mathrm{chem}} &= \mathbf W_{\mathrm{chem}}\,
        \underset{r \in \mathcal{S}_i}{\operatorname{Mean}}
        \left(\mathbf z_{ir}^{(2)}\right),\\
        \mathbf h_i^{\mathrm{sp}} &= \mathbf h_i^{\mathrm{EGNN}} +
        \Delta \mathbf h_i^{\mathrm{chem}},
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:chem_residual}
\end{equation}
where the aggregation weights the incoming messages by the pairwise
confidence $\sqrt{q_u q_v}$, continuing the pLDDT-aware weighting of
information extraction. The projection $\mathbf W_{\mathrm{chem}}$ and its
bias $\mathbf b_{\mathrm{chem}}$ are zero-initialized, so that
$\Delta \mathbf h_i^{\mathrm{chem}} = 0$ at the beginning of training. This
conservative residual design allows the new chemical-site branch to
progressively learn useful corrections from an intact geometric backbone
rather than perturbing the existing spatial representation with a randomly
initialized contribution.

Beyond these residue-level corrections, the structural expert additionally
allows multi-conformer statistics to refine the prediction through a
logit-level residual \cite{he2016deepresidual}. To avoid the high cost of
running the complete geometric network independently on every conformer,
GHXTox compresses the ensemble descriptors normalized in Section 2.4.1 into a
lightweight statistical residual: residue-level ensemble features are encoded
and pooled with both masked-mean and masked-max operations, the peptide-level
descriptor is processed by a separate encoder, and the generator identity
(PepFlow, AlphaFold2/ColabFold, or another admitted generator) is encoded by a
three-dimensional one-hot vector whose learned source embedding is
concatenated with the base structural embedding and the two ensemble
embeddings to produce a raw logit residual through a small network with a
zero-initialized final layer. The effective residual is the product of the
availability indicator and a learned weight (initialized to 0.25 and
regularized by an L1 penalty); when the availability indicator is zero, the
residual is exactly zero and the model falls back to the single-structure
prediction. The inference protocol is described in Section 2.5, and the ablation analysis in Section 3.3.

\subsubsection{pLDDT-Aware Sequence--Structure Cross-Attention}
After the EGNN and the chemical-site residual are applied, the model obtains
the chemical-enhanced spatial representation
$\mathbf H^{\mathrm{sp}} = [\mathbf h_1^{\mathrm{sp}}, \ldots,
\mathbf h_L^{\mathrm{sp}}]^{\top} \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times 128}$, where
$\mathbf h_i^{\mathrm{sp}}$ is defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:chem_residual}.
Spatial information alone does not determine whether a structural pattern is
relevant to the current sequence context: similar hydrophobic contacts may
have different implications depending on residue identities and sequence
positions. GHXTox therefore uses pLDDT-aware cross-attention within
the structural expert, allowing contextualized sequence states to selectively
retrieve spatial evidence.

A node-level gate is first constructed for each residue from the
42-dimensional residue descriptors and the pLDDT score:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        g_i^{\mathrm{base}} &= \phi_{\mathrm{node}}\left[\mathbf f_i^{\mathrm{res}} \parallel q_i\right],\\
        \mathbf c_i^{\mathrm{node}} &= \left[\frac{q_i - \mu_q}{T_q},\; \frac{(q_i - \mu_q)^2}{T_q^2},\; \mathbb{I}(q_i < 0.55)\right],\\
        r_i^{\mathrm{node}} &= 0.5 + \sigma\left(\mathbf w_n^{\top}
        \mathbf c_i^{\mathrm{node}} + b_n\right),\\
        g_i^{\mathrm{node}} &= \mathrm{clamp}\left(g_i^{\mathrm{base}}\,
        r_i^{\mathrm{node}},\; 0, 1\right),
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:node_gate}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_q = 0.70$, $T_q = 0.12$, and the residual confidence vector
$\mathbf c_i^{\mathrm{node}}$ allows the gate to adapt to the local
confidence distribution. The spatial representation is then
continuously down-weighted as
$\widehat{\mathbf H}^{\mathrm{sp}} = \mathbf H^{\mathrm{sp}} \odot
\mathbf g^{\mathrm{node}}$, with the node-level gate vector
$\mathbf g^{\mathrm{node}} \in [0,1]^L$ broadcast across the feature
dimension.

In the cross-attention operation, the contextualized sequence representation
serves as the query, and the gated spatial representation serves as the key
and value \cite{vaswani2017transformer}:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf H^{\mathrm{cross}} = \mathrm{MHA}\left(Q = \mathbf H^{\mathrm{seq}},
    K = \widehat{\mathbf H}^{\mathrm{sp}},
    V = \widehat{\mathbf H}^{\mathrm{sp}}\right).
    \label{eq:cross_attn_sp}
\end{equation}
This direction has a direct interpretation: each residue position retrieves
spatial evidence according to its contextual sequence representation. The
operation is not a direct feature interaction between the LoRA-ProtT5--Mamba
expert and the structural expert; it occurs internally between the
ESM2-enhanced sequence states and the geometric states of the structural
expert.

In addition to residue-level gating, a peptide-level gate is generated from
the overall structure-confidence profile. The model first summarizes the
profile by the mean pLDDT $\bar{q}$, the minimum pLDDT $q_{\min}$, and the
low-confidence fraction
$r_{\mathrm{low}} = \frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathbb{I}(q_i < 0.55)$, and
constructs the peptide-level confidence vector
$\mathbf c^{\mathrm{pep}} = \left[(\bar{q} - \mu_q)/T_q,\;
(q_{\min} - \mu_q)/T_q,\; r_{\mathrm{low}}\right]$. The base peptide-level
gate and its residual-adjusted form are
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        g_{\mathrm{pep}}^{\mathrm{base}} &= \phi_{\mathrm{global}}\left[\bar{q},\; q_{\min},\; r_{\mathrm{low}}\right],\\
        r_{\mathrm{pep}} &= 0.5 + \sigma\left(\mathbf w_{\mathrm{pep}}^{\top} \mathbf c^{\mathrm{pep}} + b_{\mathrm{pep}}\right),\\
        g_{\mathrm{pep}} &= \mathrm{clamp}\left(g_{\mathrm{pep}}^{\mathrm{base}}\, r_{\mathrm{pep}},\; 0, 1\right),
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:peptide_gate}
\end{equation}
where the peptide-level residual scale is analogous to, but separately
parameterized from, the node-level residual scale, and $g_{\mathrm{pep}} \in
[0,1]$ denotes the scalar peptide-level structural gate.

The final residue-level fusion representation combines the sequence state
with the gated structural context,
$\mathbf H^{\mathrm{fused}} = \operatorname{LN}\left[\mathbf H^{\mathrm{seq}}
\parallel g_{\mathrm{pep}}\, \mathbf H^{\mathrm{cross}}\right]$.
The two gating levels answer complementary questions: how reliable the
structural evidence is at an individual residue position, and how strongly the
structural branch should be trusted for the peptide as a whole. The sequence
representation is always kept, whereas the structural context is
dynamically scaled according to prediction confidence. Consequently, when the
predicted structure has low overall confidence, the structural expert can
still rely on its internal sequence-semantic pathway without allowing
uncertain coordinates to dominate the classification.

\subsubsection{Global Feature Integration and Structural Classification}
After pLDDT-aware cross-attention, each valid residue is represented by a
256-dimensional fused vector obtained by concatenating the 128-dimensional
internal sequence state with the 128-dimensional structural context. Masked
mean pooling is applied over the valid residue positions, and the pooled
vector is concatenated with five global physicochemical descriptors before a
three-layer MLP produces the base structural logit; the availability-masked
conformer correction described in Section 2.4.4 is then added, and a sigmoid
yields the final structural-expert probability
\begin{equation}
    p_{\mathrm{SE3D}} = \sigma\left(\phi_{\mathrm{cls}}\left[
    \left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{L} m_i\, \mathbf H^{\mathrm{fused}}_i}
    {\sum_{i=1}^{L} m_i}\right) \parallel
    \mathbf f_{\mathrm{pep}}^{\mathrm{phys}}\right] +
    c_{\mathrm{conf}}\, \lambda_{\mathrm{conf}}\,
    \Delta z_{\mathrm{conf}}^{\mathrm{raw}}\right),
    \label{eq:se3d_prob}
\end{equation}
where the mask excludes padding positions from the pooled representation;
$\mathbf f_{\mathrm{pep}}^{\mathrm{phys}} \in \mathbb{R}^{5}$ contains the
normalized peptide length, the length-normalized net charge, the
aromatic-residue fraction, the cysteine fraction, and the scaled mean
hydropathy; $\phi_{\mathrm{cls}}$ is a three-layer MLP with dimensions
$261 \rightarrow 128 \rightarrow 64 \rightarrow 1$, each hidden layer applying
GELU activation and dropout; and the conformer-correction term is nonzero
only when valid ensemble information is available (Section 2.4.4), so under
the benchmark and public inference protocol $c_{\mathrm{conf}} = 0$ and
$p_{\mathrm{SE3D}}$ reduces to $\sigma(z_{\mathrm{base}})$, i.e., the
single-structure base logits. Unlike maximum pooling, masked mean pooling
integrates structural evidence distributed across residue positions, which is
appropriate when toxicity-related spatial patterns are not confined to a
single local region. The resulting probability is passed directly to the
dual-expert fusion in Section 2.5; the standalone structural threshold is used
only for branch-specific analyses.
\subsection{Fusion and Final Output}
The 1D expert emits the OOF-calibrated probability $p_{\mathrm{1D}}$
(Section 2.3), and the structural expert emits $p_{\mathrm{SE3D}}$
(Section 2.4). The two probabilities are fused linearly in probability space
with weights frozen on training out-of-fold (OOF) predictions:
\begin{equation}
    p_{\mathrm{GHXTox}} = 0.72\,p_{\mathrm{1D}} + 0.28\,p_{\mathrm{SE3D}},
    \label{eq:fusion}
\end{equation}
The fused probability $p_{\mathrm{GHXTox}}$ is the model's continuous
toxicity-probability output; it is binarized directly at the OOF-frozen
threshold, predicting toxic ($\hat y = 1$) if
$p_{\mathrm{GHXTox}} \geq 0.47022256531284556$ and non-toxic ($\hat y = 0$)
otherwise. The default output is therefore $(p_{\mathrm{GHXTox}}, \hat y)$.
Experiments showed that directly emitting the fused probability yields the
best overall performance, so no additional network or calibration is applied
after fusion.

The two experts were trained independently because they differ in input
representation, parameter scale, and optimization behavior (the 1D and 3D
loss, calibration, and training details are described in Sections 2.3 and
2.4). Group-aware training OOF predictions were used for calibration,
fusion-weight selection, and threshold determination, and Test1/Test2 labels
were excluded from all of these procedures. The training sequences were
clustered by CD-HIT at a sequence-identity threshold of 0.80, with all
peptides in the same homology group confined to the same fold; each fold was
predicted by a model trained only on its complement, giving every training
peptide a single OOF probability independent of its own optimization. Both
experts were optimized with AdamW \cite{loshchilov2019adamw}. The fusion
weight and decision threshold were selected by maximizing MCC on the complete
training OOF table (with BACC as the secondary tie-break criterion and a
leave-one-fold-out check), yielding $w_{\mathrm{1D}}=0.72$,
$w_{\mathrm{SE3D}}=0.28$, and $\tau = 0.47022256531284556$; these values
were then frozen and applied unchanged to Test1 and Test2. In the final
benchmark and public inference protocol, the conformer availability mask is
fixed to zero and the structural probability is read from the
single-structure base logits.
\subsection{Evaluation Criteria}
Given the class-imbalanced benchmarks (Test2 has 46 toxic and 536 non-toxic
peptides), MCC and BACC served as the primary threshold-dependent metrics,
with ACC, sensitivity (SN), specificity (SP), precision, and F1 reported for
completeness; AUROC/AUPRC quantified ranking performance, and Brier/ECE-10
assessed probability quality. Threshold-dependent metrics used the fixed
OOF-derived threshold (Section 2.5); ranking and calibration metrics used
the continuous probabilities. Binary predictions
$\hat{y}_i = \mathbb{I}[p_i \geq \tau]$ were summarized by
$\mathrm{ACC}=(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{TN})/N$,
$\mathrm{SN}=\mathrm{TP}/(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN})$,
$\mathrm{SP}=\mathrm{TN}/(\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP})$,
$F_1=2\mathrm{TP}/(2\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{FN})$, and
$\mathrm{BACC}=(\mathrm{SN}+\mathrm{SP})/2$. MCC \cite{matthews1975mcc},
Brier \cite{brier1950}, and ten-bin ECE-10 (final bin includes $p_i = 1$) are
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathrm{MCC} &= \frac{\mathrm{TP}\,\mathrm{TN} -
        \mathrm{FP}\,\mathrm{FN}}
        {\sqrt{(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP})(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN})
        (\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP})(\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FN})}},\\
        \mathrm{Brier} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(p_i - y_i)^2,\\
        \mathrm{ECE}_{10} &= \sum_{m=1}^{10}\frac{|\mathcal{B}_m|}{N}\,
        \left|\mathrm{conf}(\mathcal{B}_m) -
        \mathrm{freq}(\mathcal{B}_m)\right|,
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:metrics}
\end{equation}
where TP/TN/FP/FN are the true/false positive/negative counts, $y_i$ the
labels, and $\mathcal{B}_m$ the $m$-th equal-width bin over $[0,1]$ (mean
confidence $\mathrm{conf}(\mathcal{B}_m)$ vs. observed toxic frequency
$\mathrm{freq}(\mathcal{B}_m)$); ECE bins stayed fixed across models and
datasets.
\section{RESULTS AND DISCUSSION}
\subsection{Overall Predictive Performance on the Benchmark Test Sets}
Using the OOF-frozen weight and threshold without dataset-specific retuning,
GHXTox correctly classified 1{,}067 of 1{,}126 Test1 peptides
(TP/TN/FP/FN = 287/780/26/33) and 551 of 582 strongly imbalanced Test2
peptides (35/516/20/11); the complete metric set is listed in
Table~\ref{tab:overall_comparison}. The low Test2 BACC (0.8618) and F1
(0.6931), despite high accuracy, reflect the difficulty of recovering the 46
toxic peptides and demonstrate why class-balanced and precision--recall
metrics are essential for this benchmark.

Table~\ref{tab:overall_comparison} also reports previously published models on
the same benchmark partitions (ToxMSRC and ToxPLTC; values transcribed from
the original publications, and NR denotes an unreported metric)
\cite{zhang2025toxmsrc,liang2026toxpltc}. GHXTox obtains the highest Test1
ACC, BACC, SP, MCC, AUROC, and AUPRC among the compared rows, whereas ToxPLTC
has higher Test1 SN; on Test2, GHXTox obtains the highest ACC, SP, MCC, and
reported AUPRC, but its BACC and SN remain below both reference models. This
reflects a stronger overall balance of specificity, correlation, and
positive-class ranking rather than comprehensive superiority on every metric.
Because Test1 and Test2 were observed historically, these differences remain
retrospective benchmark evidence and require confirmation on a new prefrozen
external set.

\subsection{Robustness, Statistical Uncertainty, and Applicability Analysis}
Point estimates alone are insufficient for distinguishing a stable model
improvement from variation caused by finite samples. We therefore use
multi-seed averaging for the 1D expert and paired, sample-level bootstrap
resampling for model comparisons, all performed on the frozen predictor
without retraining, recalibration, or threshold adjustment. Three full-data
1D models (seeds 42, 123, and 2025) are averaged before calibration; this
ensemble is the final predictor, with no seed selected according to Test1 or
Test2 performance, and a 15-member fold-checkpoint ensemble was examined but
not adopted because it did not improve the frozen validation criteria.

For risk-aware screening, prediction risk was quantified by the
decision-boundary proximity $d_i = 1 - |p_i - \tau|/\max(\tau, 1-\tau)$ with
$\tau = 0.47022256531284556$. On training OOF this proximity provides an
informative ranking: as coverage decreases from 100\% to 80\%, accepted-sample
error decreases and MCC increases monotonically, whereas equal-count random
rejection shows no comparable improvement; at the prespecified 90\% operating
point the cutoff was frozen at $d_{\mathrm{OOF}}^{*} = 0.47361765874159856$
(Figure~\ref{fig:selective_prediction}). Transferring this cutoff unchanged
to Test1 and Test2 reduced the accepted-sample error by about 2.2--2.5
percentage points and improved MCC and BACC, while equal-count random
rejection retained the full-coverage error (Table~\ref{tab:selective_transfer}).
The transfer uses no benchmark-specific quantile, coverage, class prevalence,
or label, so the naturally different realized coverages reflect a single
OOF-derived cutoff rather than retuned abstention rates. This supports a
risk-aware screening workflow in which boundary-proximal predictions are
flagged for review, although it does not constitute independent validation,
and the original binary prediction remains available for every abstained
peptide.

\subsection{Interpretability and Representative Case Studies}
The interpretation experiments used a prespecified case-selection rule: within
each test set and each expert-outcome group, one toxic and one non-toxic
peptide nearest the fusion threshold were chosen (ties resolved by source
index), forming a 16-peptide cohort. Before perturbation, baseline
probabilities were recomputed and compared with the stored prediction vectors;
the maximum absolute discrepancy for the 1D expert within the case cohort was
$3.45\times10^{-4}$, and the corresponding 3D discrepancies were
$5.49\times10^{-4}$ on Test1 and $1.14\times10^{-5}$ on Test2, none of which
changed any decision. The interpretation analyses therefore describe model
sensitivity on the frozen predictor rather than confirmed biological
mechanisms.

Mean pLDDT was strongly associated with the learned global structural gate
(Spearman 0.960 on Test1, 0.950 on Test2, pooled 0.957), which can be read as
the model preferring to trust structural evidence when the predicted structure
is more confident, systematically down-weighting structural evidence for
flexible or disordered short peptides, consistent with their conformational
heterogeneity. Across 445 individual site deletions, most changes were small
(median 0.00048, mean 0.00997, 95th percentile 0.057, maximum 0.2994 in
$p_{3D}$); hydrophobic and aromatic sites showed the largest mean effects
(0.126 and 0.0687, respectively) and sulfur-containing sites the smallest
(0.00179), suggesting that hydrophobic patches and aromatic interactions
(aromatic stacking, cation--$\pi$) may be among the more informative
spatial-chemical features for peptide toxicity, consistent with the prevalence
of hydrophobic and aromatic residues in membrane-active or toxic peptides (a
hypothesis requiring experimental validation). The opposing directions of the
largest deletion effects (Phe8 increased $p_{3D}$, Pro19 decreased it) show
that deletion scores depend on the surrounding peptide context and cannot be
read as an intrinsic toxicity label for a functional group
(Figure~\ref{fig:structural_sensitivity}).

Fixed late fusion agreed on most benchmark peptides, and the discordant cases
illustrate its operational consequences (the aggregate pattern is reported in
Section 3.2). The toxic peptide \texttt{sequence\_23\_23} (ACLDIGNSCRED, two
cysteines) had a weak 1D probability of 0.2806 but a high-confidence 3D
probability of 0.9954; fusion raised the probability to 0.4808 and restored
the correct toxic classification, suggesting that the toxicity signal of this
short peptide may depend more on spatial conformation and the cysteine
backbone than on linear sequence features. The toxic peptide
\texttt{sequence\_34\_34} had a correct 1D probability of 0.5447 but a 3D
probability of $3\times10^{-5}$, and fusion reduced the probability to 0.3922,
producing a false negative; strong sequence evidence can therefore be
overridden when the structural expert supplies sufficiently strong opposing
evidence, and such single-sample evidence conflicts warrant further
inspection (Figure~\ref{fig:expert_disagreement_cases}). These cases confirm
the design premise of the model: peptide toxicity depends not only on the
linear sequence but also on three-dimensional conformation as a key source of
evidence, with the two types of evidence sometimes consistent and sometimes
conflicting; fixed late fusion lets the two dimensions complement each other,
which is why the 1D sequence and 3D conformation are placed together and fused
at the probability level rather than relying on a single dimension.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Directions}
The most fundamental limitation stems from the single-output nature of
three-dimensional conformation prediction. Short peptides in solution
typically populate multiple interconverting conformations, and toxicity is
usually mediated by one particular conformation or class of conformations; a
single predicted structure cannot cover this multimodal distribution. This is
precisely why the project introduced multiple conformation generators
(PepFlow and AlphaFold2/ColabFold, assigned by length range). However,
different generators exhibit inherent noise, and normalizing conformers
across models and sources further amplifies this inconsistency: activating
the multi-conformer residual on the test sets produced negligible and
inconsistent effects that did not justify a public inference protocol, and
source-independent and fusion-aware candidates also failed their prespecified
retention gates (Section 3.3, Tables~\ref{tab:conformer_ablation_test1} and
\ref{tab:conformer_ablation_test2}), so the conformer residual was eventually
disabled for benchmark and public inference and the structural
information-extraction capacity of the model was not fully released. On
training OOF the residual improved MCC by only $+0.00190$ ($p = 0.203$, not
significant), and expanding the conformer cohort from 250 to 761 peptides did
not pass the retention gates because the added records were all negative. In
addition, full-atom conformational sampling (e.g., PepFlow) is computationally
far more expensive than single-structure prediction, which limited large-scale
generation and deployment. We therefore hope that next-generation protein
structure prediction models can provide, at acceptable computational cost,
multiple possible conformations and their probabilities (for example,
lightweight multi-conformer sampling based on flow matching or diffusion),
more closely matching the multimodal nature of peptide conformation and
potentially further releasing the value of conformational information in
toxicity prediction.

The second limitation is that the 1D sequence and 3D conformation dimensions
are combined with a fixed 0.72/0.28 weight rather than dynamically adjusted
per sample. This may partly stem from the first limitation: the inherent noise
among different conformation predictors masks the information that would
indicate which dimension should be trusted for a given sample, and when
structural evidence is itself inconsistent across generators, the model cannot
reliably distinguish, per sample, the reliability of sequence evidence from
that of conformational evidence and therefore falls back to fixed weights.
Consistent with this, sample-level confidence gates and dynamic routing
attempts did not pass the retention gates: the former produced MCC values
below the fixed default, and the latter improved ranking and calibration but
did not exceed the frozen default's decision metrics (Section 3.4). This
reflects that the two dimensions have not yet achieved sufficiently effective
information complementarity; adaptive and interpretable fusion based on
evidence reliability, together with more consistent conformational evidence
sources, are directions for future work.
\section{CONCLUSIONS}
GHXTox explores the complementary joint use of sequence and conformational
evidence by combining a task-adaptive LoRA-ProtT5--Bi-Mamba sequence expert
with a pLDDT-aware multi-scale structural expert through probability-level
fusion; the two experts are independent and their complementarity is
characterized by the structural expert correcting a subset of sequence errors
in a class-asymmetric way (Section 3.2). The structural expert integrates residue geometry, full-atom-derived
chemical sites, and sequence-conditioned structural evidence, while
multi-conformer information is restricted to training
and controlled ablation. The final predictor uses the structural expert's
single-structure base probability. The 1D/3D
weights (0.72/0.28) and the decision threshold (0.47022256531284556) were
fixed from training OOF predictions before benchmark evaluation.

On the frozen historical Test1 and Test2 benchmarks, the model reaches MCC
values of 0.8705 and 0.6673 and AUPRC values of 0.9674 and 0.8046, and the
final fusion improves AUROC, AUPRC, and the Brier score relative to the
calibrated 1D stream. The 3D expert correctly classified 36.92\% and 37.50\%
of the respective Test1 and Test2 errors made by the 1D expert, with most of
these structural rescues occurring among non-toxic peptides. An OOF-frozen
selective policy further reduced accepted-sample error to 2.71\% and 3.16\%
at realized coverages of 88.54\% and 86.94\%, respectively, without
benchmark-specific cutoff selection. Conformer-derived information produced
only modest and non-uniform improvements, indicating that mapping
conformational variation to toxicity-relevant evidence remains an important
limitation. The interpretability analyses describe model sensitivity rather
than causal mechanisms.

Several limitations remain, most notably the single-output nature of
conformation prediction and the fixed probability-level fusion between the
two dimensions, as discussed in Section 3.6; both benchmarks were also
observed during earlier model development, and the structures are predicted
rather than experimental. Looking ahead, we hope that more powerful
three-dimensional conformation models, capable of producing multiple
conformations with their probabilities at acceptable computational cost,
together with dynamic, evidence-reliability-aware fusion between the sequence
and structural dimensions, will enable a deeper complementary joint use of
the two types of information, with experimental validation where feasible.
