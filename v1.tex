\begin{abstract}
Peptide toxicity is a major safety concern in peptide-based drug development,
motivating accurate computational screening methods. Existing methods have
begun to explore multi-dimensional (multimodal) feature fusion and toxicity
prediction based on the three-dimensional (3D) conformations of peptides,
yet most of them still underutilize 3D structural information, and
systematic multi-dimensional fusion remains rare. Here, we propose GHXTox, a
hierarchical dual-level fusion framework in which a 3D structural expert
first integrates sequence semantics with spatial structural features through
predicted local-distance difference test (pLDDT)-aware cross-attention, and
is then fused at the probability level with a task-adaptive one-dimensional
(1D) sequence expert using weights frozen on training out-of-fold (OOF)
predictions. The 1D branch analyzes sequences with a protein language model
(ProtT5 fine-tuned with low-rank adaptation, LoRA) and a bidirectional Mamba
state-space model. The 3D expert integrates an ESMFold-derived structural
anchor, pLDDT-aware residue geometry, and full-atom-derived chemical sites.
Multi-conformer information is retained only in training-side representation
learning and controlled ablations; benchmark and public inference always use
the single-structure base logits. On the frozen historical benchmark test sets,
GHXTox achieves MCC values of 0.8705 and 0.6673 and AUPRC values of 0.9674 and
0.8046 on Test1 and Test2, respectively. Further analyses characterize
residue-level sequence sensitivity, confidence--gate associations,
chemical-site perturbations, expert disagreement, and an OOF-frozen
selective-prediction rule. At realized coverages of 88.54\% and 86.94\%, the
selective rule reduced the accepted-sample error to 2.71\% and 3.16\% on
Test1 and Test2 without benchmark-specific cutoff selection. These findings
are interpreted as model-level evidence rather than causal biological mechanisms.
Frozen-model analyses indicate that the conformer contribution is small and
non-uniform: under the structural expert, substantial geometric
variation does not necessarily translate into stable outcome-aligned
probability variation.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} peptide toxicity prediction; multimodal deep learning; protein language model;
bidirectional Mamba; pLDDT-aware multi-scale 3D modeling; interpretability

\section{INTRODUCTION}

Peptides are increasingly used as therapeutic candidates because of their
high target specificity and functional diversity
\cite{muttenthaler2021}. However, peptide toxicity remains a major safety
concern during drug development, and the accurate and efficient
identification of toxic peptides is a major bottleneck in peptide-based drug
discovery \cite{zhang2025toxmsrc}. Because conventional experimental
toxicity assays are costly, time-consuming, and difficult to scale, reliable
computational predictors are highly desirable.

Computational peptide toxicity prediction has evolved from traditional machine
learning methods based on handcrafted physicochemical features toward deep
learning approaches with stronger representation-learning capability. Current
mainstream methods can be roughly grouped into two categories. The first
category operates on sequence-level (one-dimensional, 1D) and/or atomic-level
(two-dimensional, 2D) representations. For example, ToxMSRC combines
continuous bag-of-words (CBOW) embeddings, multi-scale convolutional neural
networks, and a bidirectional long short-term memory network with residual
connections, achieving strong performance on the benchmark dataset \cite{zhang2025toxmsrc}.
ToxPLTC integrates pooled ProtT5 embeddings, borderline-SMOTE augmentation,
and a text convolutional neural network \cite{liang2026toxpltc}. ToxiPep fuses context-aware
sequence representations with atomic-level graphs through a cross-attention
mechanism \cite{guan2025toxipep}. HyPepTox-Fuse further combines embeddings from multiple protein
language models with conventional descriptors \cite{tran2025hypeptoxfuse}. Although these models
achieve strong performance within their respective modalities, they largely
neglect the three-dimensional (3D) conformations of peptides, which are
closely related to toxicity; cross-dimensional information interaction and
mutual validation remain limited, and their interpretability is mostly
confined to the sequence level.

The second category attempts to exploit 3D structural information. PeptiTox
uses ESM2 sequence embeddings together with ESMFold-predicted 3D structures
and geometric graph neural networks \cite{wang2025peptitox}. StrucToxNet feeds ProtT5 sequence
embeddings and ESMFold-predicted structures into an equivariant graph neural
network (EGNN) and systematically analyzes structural quality \cite{jiao2025structoxnet}. These
studies demonstrate the value of 3D conformations for toxicity prediction.
However, such methods are constrained by the limited accuracy of current
structure prediction models on short peptides and typically rely on a single
predicted conformation; structural confidence, i.e., the predicted local
distance difference test (pLDDT) score, is either discarded through hard
filtering or ignored, and multi-conformer ensembles are not considered.
Moreover, the fusion between the sequence and structure branches remains
relatively shallow, and no systematic multi-dimensional fusion that combines a
task-adaptive sequence model with confidence-aware multi-scale structure
modeling has been established.

To address these limitations, we propose GHXTox, an interpretable hierarchical
dual-level fusion framework for peptide toxicity prediction. A task-adaptive
1D sequence expert combines a ProtT5 protein language model, adapted by
low-rank adaptation (LoRA), with a bidirectional Mamba state-space encoder
and three-seed ensembling \cite{elnaggar2022prottrans,hu2022lora,gu2024mamba}.
A sequence-enhanced 3D
structural expert integrates sequence semantics with spatial structure
through pLDDT-aware cross-attention, ESMFold-derived geometry, full-atom
chemical sites, and confidence gating across multiple structural scales;
PepFlow and AlphaFold2/ColabFold conformer ensembles informed only
training-side analyses and controlled ablations, and the final predictor uses
the single-structure ESMFold anchor
\cite{lin2023esmfold,jumper2021alphafold2,mirdita2022colabfold,abdin2024pepflow}.
The two expert probabilities are fused at the probability level with weights
fixed on training out-of-fold (OOF) predictions; the 1D probabilities are
first calibrated on the same OOF table, and no benchmark labels are used
during this selection. Gated sequence--structure
cross-attention inside the structural expert further informs the interaction
design \cite{zhang2026neuroppredpscg}. Ablation experiments show that the
contribution of structural evidence is dataset- and metric-dependent: fusion
improves several fixed-threshold and probability-quality measures on Test1
while maintaining broadly comparable performance on Test2. Post hoc analyses
characterize residue-level sensitivity, confidence gating, chemical-site
perturbations, and expert disagreement without assigning causal biological
meaning to these signals. The framework is evaluated on the frozen historical
benchmarks described in Section 2.1.

In summary, the main contributions of this work are threefold: (1) we propose
GHXTox, an interpretable hierarchical dual-level fusion framework that integrates a
task-adaptive 1D protein language model with pLDDT-aware multi-scale 3D
structure modeling; (2) the 3D branch designs a multi-scale structural
information extraction strategy spanning from local residue functional groups
to the whole peptide, and incorporates pLDDT confidence into structural
feature processing through continuous down-weighting and dynamic gating; and
(3) extensive experiments on the frozen historical benchmark test sets show
that GHXTox improves several fixed-threshold and probability-quality measures
on Test1 while maintaining broadly comparable performance on Test2, while
ablation, robustness, and interpretability analyses characterize the
conditions under which sequence and structural evidence are complementary.

\subsection{Datasets}
We evaluate GHXTox on the benchmark datasets constructed by CAPTP \cite{jiao2024captp} and
subsequently adopted by ToxMSRC \cite{zhang2025toxmsrc}. Following the data construction protocol
of ToxMSRC \cite{zhang2025toxmsrc}, toxic peptide sequences were merged from three databases,
namely CSM-Toxin \cite{morozov2023csmboxin}, ToxinPred2 \cite{sharma2022toxinpred2}, and ATSE \cite{wei2021atse}, and sequences longer
than 50 residues were removed. Non-toxic sequences were retrieved from UniProt
\cite{uniprot2019} using the query ``NOT KW-0800 AND NOT KW-0020 AND reviewed:true'', and
sequences containing non-standard amino acids or exceeding 50 residues were
excluded. After combining the two groups and removing samples with conflicting
labels, CD-HIT \cite{fu2012cdhit} was applied with a sequence-identity threshold of 0.9 to
eliminate redundant sequences, yielding 2{,}138 toxic and 5{,}375 non-toxic
peptides. Following the same partition as ToxMSRC \cite{zhang2025toxmsrc}, we used 85\% of the data
as the training set and the remaining 15\% as the benchmark Test1 set,
resulting in 1{,}818 toxic and
4{,}569 non-toxic training peptides and 320 toxic and 806 non-toxic Test1
peptides. An additional benchmark Test2 set, containing 46 toxic and 536
non-toxic peptides, is used for frozen historical evaluation. Both Test1 and
Test2 had been observed during historical project development and are
therefore treated as frozen historical benchmarks rather than newly blinded
external validation sets; their labels were not used in the formal OOF
calibration, fusion-weight selection, or threshold-freezing procedure.
ToxinPred~3.0 \cite{rathore2024toxinpred3} was consulted as an external method
and data-source reference during project development, but its records were not
added to the present training set or to the formal Test1/Test2 result tables.
Table~\ref{tab:datasets} summarizes the statistics of the datasets used in
this study.

\subsection{Overview of the Model}
GHXTox is a hierarchical dual-level fusion framework for peptide toxicity
prediction: a task-adaptive one-dimensional (1D) sequence expert and a
sequence-enhanced three-dimensional (3D) structural expert generate
independent probabilities, interact internally at the feature level, and are
combined at the probability level.

    \caption{Overall architecture of GHXTox. The framework combines a
             task-adaptive 1D sequence expert with a pLDDT-aware structural
             expert through probability-level fusion. The conformer residual
             is restricted to training-side analysis and is disabled during
             benchmark and public inference.}
    \label{fig:overview}

The 1D expert processes the peptide sequence with a LoRA-adapted ProtT5
encoder \cite{elnaggar2022prottrans,hu2022lora} followed by a bidirectional
Mamba state-space model \cite{gu2024mamba}, averages three seed models, and
applies OOF-fitted Platt calibration to produce $p_{\mathrm{1D}}$
(Section 2.3). The 3D expert integrates sequence semantics, residue
geometry, full-atom chemical sites, and pLDDT confidence at five scales,
combines sequence and spatial states through pLDDT-aware cross-attention,
and emits $p_{\mathrm{SE3D}}$ (Section 2.4).

The two expert probabilities are fused linearly in probability space with
weights fixed on training out-of-fold (OOF) predictions; the 0.72/0.28
weights are an empirical OOF-selection outcome, not a manually assigned
prior:
\begin{equation}
    p_{\mathrm{GHXTox}} = 0.72\,p_{\mathrm{1D}} + 0.28\,p_{\mathrm{SE3D}}.
    \label{eq:fusion}
\end{equation}
The fused probability is converted into a binary prediction with the same
OOF-fixed threshold:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y} = \begin{cases}
        1, & p_{\mathrm{GHXTox}} \geq 0.4702225653,\\
        0, & p_{\mathrm{GHXTox}} < 0.4702225653,
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:decision}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{y}=1$ denotes a toxic peptide and $\hat{y}=0$ a non-toxic
peptide. These parameters are applied unchanged to both benchmarks
(Section 2.5.4).

\subsection{Task-Adaptive 1D Sequence Modeling}
The 1D sequence expert is designed to learn toxicity-related contextual
semantics and sequential dependencies directly from peptide amino acid
sequences. Instead of reducing a frozen protein-language-model output to a
fixed mean vector, GHXTox preserves the residue-level representations
generated by ProtT5 and adapts the pretrained encoder to the peptide toxicity
prediction task through low-rank adaptation (LoRA). The resulting
residue-level representations are then processed by a bidirectional Mamba
state-space encoder to jointly model local residue patterns, N-to-C
dependencies, and C-to-N dependencies. The encoded residue features are
aggregated via masked mean pooling and passed to a linear classification head
to produce the single-model toxicity probability. Finally, three models
trained on the full training set with different random seeds are combined by
equal-weight probability averaging, followed by Platt calibration fitted
exclusively on training out-of-fold (OOF) predictions, yielding the final 1D
expert probability $p_{\mathrm{1D}}$.

\subsubsection{Residue Tokenization and LoRA-Adapted ProtT5}
Given a peptide sequence of length $L$, residues are converted into the
space-separated format required by the ProtT5 tokenizer and mapped to token
indices; sequences of different lengths are padded within each batch. A
binary residue-validity mask distinguishes residue positions from special
and padded tokens and is applied in the Mamba scans and subsequent pooling.

The tokenized sequence is processed by the ProtT5-XL-UniRef50 encoder
\cite{elnaggar2022prottrans} with LoRA, producing residue-level
representations $\mathbf H^{\mathrm{PLM}} \in \mathbb{R}^{L\times1024}$.
Retaining the complete residue-level representation matrix preserves
position-specific contextual semantics for the downstream encoder; each
residue vector encodes both its amino acid identity and its context within
the peptide sequence.

To adapt the general-purpose encoder to the toxicity task, low-rank
adaptation (LoRA) \cite{hu2022lora} is applied to the linear transformations
within the encoder. For an original linear layer
$\mathbf y=\mathbf W_0\mathbf x+\mathbf b$, the pretrained weight matrix
$\mathbf W_0$ is frozen, and the task-specific update is factorized into two
trainable low-rank matrices:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf W
=
\mathbf W_0+
\frac{\alpha}{r}\mathbf B\mathbf A,
\qquad
\mathbf A\in\mathbb R^{r\times d_{\mathrm{in}}},
\quad
\mathbf B\in\mathbb R^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r}.
\label{eq:lora_update}
\end{equation}
We set the LoRA rank to $r=8$, the scaling coefficient to $\alpha=16$, and
the LoRA dropout rate to $0.1$, yielding a scaling factor of $\alpha/r=2$.
Matrix $\mathbf A$ is initialized with Kaiming initialization, whereas
$\mathbf B$ is initialized to zero, so the LoRA branch initially leaves the
pretrained transformation unchanged. In contrast to configurations that
apply LoRA only to selected attention projections, all linear layers within
the ProtT5 encoder are augmented; the pretrained base transformations and
embeddings remain frozen, whereas the low-rank parameters and the downstream
sequence-classification modules are trainable.

The 1024-dimensional ProtT5 representations are then projected into the
192-dimensional hidden space used by the Mamba encoder \cite{gu2024mamba}:
\begin{equation*}
\mathbf X^{(0)}
=
\operatorname{Dropout}
\left(
\operatorname{GELU}
\left[
\operatorname{LN}
\left(
\mathbf H^{\mathrm{PLM}}\mathbf W_p+\mathbf b_p
\right)
\right]
\right),
\qquad
\mathbf X^{(0)}\in\mathbb R^{L\times192}.
\end{equation*}
where $\operatorname{LN}$ denotes layer normalization.

\subsubsection{Bidirectional Mamba Sequence Encoder}
Although ProtT5 provides contextualized residue representations, these
representations primarily encode general protein semantics learned during
large-scale pretraining. To further model the sequential dependencies
specifically associated with peptide toxicity, two bidirectional Mamba layers
are placed after the ProtT5 projection block. Each directional block follows
the standard selective state-space formulation of Mamba
\cite{gu2024mamba}: the input is projected into content and gating branches,
processed by a depthwise convolution, discretized with input-dependent step
sizes, and updated through a recurrent state of dimension 16. In compact
form, the discretized transition and the state update are
\begin{equation}
    \bar{\mathbf A}_t = \exp(\Delta_t \mathbf A), \qquad
    \mathbf S_t = \bar{\mathbf A}_t \odot \mathbf S_{t-1} + \bar{\mathbf B}_t
    \odot \widetilde{\mathbf U}_t^{\mathrm{m}},
    \label{eq:mamba_selective}
\end{equation}
followed by a gated output projection. At padding positions, the convolved
feature and the step size are zeroed, preventing padding tokens from
injecting information into the recurrent states.

A unidirectional scan accesses only one side of the sequence context, whereas
peptide toxicity may depend on residue patterns associated with both termini
and with interactions spanning different sequence positions. In the
$\ell$-th bidirectional layer, forward and backward scans are therefore
applied to the normalized sequence using the residue-validity mask
$\mathbf m$, and the two directional outputs are concatenated and fused by a
linear layer with a residual connection:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathbf F^{(\ell)} &= \mathcal{M}_f^{(\ell)}(\operatorname{LN}(\mathbf X^{(\ell)}), \mathbf m),\\
        \mathbf R^{(\ell)} &= \operatorname{Reverse}\left[\mathcal{M}_b^{(\ell)}
        \left(\operatorname{Reverse}\left[\operatorname{LN}(\mathbf X^{(\ell)})\right],
        \operatorname{Reverse}(\mathbf m)\right)\right],
        \\
        \mathbf X^{(\ell+1)} &= \mathbf X^{(\ell)} + \operatorname{Dropout}\left(
        \operatorname{GELU}\left(\mathbf W_m \left[\mathbf F^{(\ell)} \parallel
        \mathbf R^{(\ell)}\right] + \mathbf b_m\right)\right),
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:mamba_bidir}
\end{equation}
where $\parallel$ denotes feature concatenation and the backward-scan output
is restored to the original residue order. Two such layers are stacked in the
final model, allowing each residue position to receive conditional information
from both its N-terminal and C-terminal contexts.

\subsubsection{Masked Pooling and Class-Weighted Classification}
After two bidirectional Mamba layers, the model produces residue-level
representations $\mathbf X^{(2)} \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times 192}$. Because
peptide sequences have variable lengths, GHXTox does not rely on a
fixed-position classification token. Instead, masked mean pooling is applied
over the valid amino acid positions:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf h_{1D} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{L} m_i \mathbf x_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{L} m_i},
    \label{eq:masked_pool}
\end{equation}
where the denominator equals the number of valid residue positions. This
operation converts the variable-length residue sequence into a fixed
192-dimensional peptide-level representation while ensuring that EOS and
padding positions do not contribute to the pooled feature; unlike global max
pooling, it aggregates distributed evidence across the entire peptide.

The pooled representation is normalized and passed through a single-output
linear classification head,
$z_{1D} = \mathbf w_c^{\top} \operatorname{LN}(\mathbf h_{1D}) + b_c$, giving
the uncalibrated toxicity probability $p_{1D}^{\mathrm{raw}} = \sigma(z_{1D})$.
The classification head therefore consists of layer normalization followed by
a single linear layer, rather than a multilayer perceptron.

Because non-toxic peptides substantially outnumber toxic peptides in the
training data, a positive-class-weighted binary cross-entropy loss is used in
the current 1D expert instead of SMOTE or Borderline-SMOTE. For a training
partition containing $N_+$ toxic and $N_-$ non-toxic peptides, the
positive-class weight is $w_+ = N_-/N_+$, and the class-weighted objective is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{WBCE}} = -\frac{1}{N}
    \sum_{j=1}^{N} \left[ w_+ y_j \log \sigma(z_j)
    + (1 - y_j) \log(1 - \sigma(z_j)) \right].
    \label{eq:wbce}
\end{equation}
During five-fold out-of-fold training, $w_+$ is calculated independently from
the class counts of each fold's training partition; for full-data training, it
is recalculated using the complete training set. This strategy increases the
contribution of the minority toxic class without generating synthetic peptide
sequences or feature vectors, while preventing any information from the
held-out validation fold from entering the training procedure.

\subsubsection{Three-Seed Ensemble and OOF Platt Calibration}
The predictions of deep neural networks may vary because of parameter
initialization, mini-batch ordering, and stochastic optimization. To reduce
the variance associated with an individual training run, three 1D expert
models are trained under identical data, architecture, and optimization
settings using the random seeds $42$, $123$, and $2025$. The final ensemble
consists of three models trained on the complete training set, rather than
the fold-specific checkpoints produced during cross-validation.

For sample $j$, the toxicity probability produced by the model trained with
seed $k$ is denoted by $p_j^{(k)} = \sigma(z_j^{(k)})$, $k \in \{1,2,3\}$, and
the uncalibrated ensemble score is obtained by equal-weight arithmetic
averaging:
\begin{equation}
    s_j = \frac{1}{3} \sum_{k=1}^{3} p_j^{(k)}.
    \label{eq:ensemble_score}
\end{equation}
No individual seed is selected according to its performance on Test1 or
Test2, and no seed-specific ensemble weight is introduced.

Although probability averaging reduces prediction variance, neural-network
probabilities may remain systematically overconfident or underconfident.
Platt scaling \cite{platt1999} is therefore applied after the three-model
probability average. Training OOF predictions are first generated using the
fixed group-aware five-fold partition; each training peptide receives an OOF
probability from a model trained without the fold containing that peptide,
and the OOF probabilities from the three random seeds are averaged to obtain
the OOF ensemble score $s_j^{\mathrm{OOF}}$. A two-parameter logistic
calibrator is fitted to these OOF scores:
\begin{equation}
    p_{1D,j} = \sigma\left(a\, \mathrm{logit}\left(\widetilde{s}_j\right) + b\right),
    \label{eq:platt_form}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{logit}(s) = \ln(s/(1-s))$,
$\widetilde{s}_j = \mathrm{clip}(s_j, \varepsilon, 1-\varepsilon)$ with
$\varepsilon = 10^{-6}$, and $a$ and $b$ denote the Platt calibration
parameters; the clip prevents infinite logits for probabilities of exactly
0 or 1, and the same clipping is applied during fitting. The parameters are
estimated by minimizing binary cross-entropy on the OOF scores, and the scale
is reparameterized as $a = \mathrm{clip}(\exp(\eta),\, 0.02, 50) > 0$, which
guarantees a monotonic transformation and therefore preserves the sample
ranking of the ensemble score. For benchmark test or previously unseen
peptides, the frozen calibrator is applied to the three-seed average:
$p_{\mathrm{1D}} = \sigma\left(a\, \mathrm{logit}\left(\mathrm{clip}
\left(\frac{1}{3} \sum_{k=1}^{3} p^{(k)},\; \varepsilon,\;
1-\varepsilon\right)\right) + b\right)$. The labels of Test1 and Test2 are
not used for ensemble-member selection, calibration fitting, or 1D threshold
optimization. The calibrated probability $p_{\mathrm{1D}}$ is the
sequence-expert output used in the top-level probability fusion described in
Section 2.2; no additional Platt calibrator is fitted after the final
GHXTox probability fusion.

\subsection{pLDDT-Aware Multi-Scale 3D Structural Modeling}
Peptide toxicity is influenced not only by amino acid composition and linear
residue order but also by the three-dimensional (3D) arrangement of charged
groups, hydrophobic regions, aromatic systems, and sulfur-containing
moieties. However, peptides are generally short, conformationally flexible,
and poorly covered by experimentally determined structures. Treating a single
predicted conformation as an error-free structural observation may therefore
amplify structure-prediction errors. Conversely, representing a peptide with
only C$\alpha$ coordinates or peptide-level summary descriptors fails to capture the
local spatial-chemical environments that are relevant to toxicity.

To address these limitations, GHXTox employs a pLDDT-aware multi-scale
structural expert that captures complementary information at five levels:
residue semantics, spatial residue neighborhoods, full-atom chemical sites,
peptide-level global properties, and conformer-ensemble statistics. The model
first extracts residue-level C$\alpha$ coordinates, full-atom coordinates, and
normalized pLDDT confidence scores from the predicted structure. An internal
sequence-semantic subbranch combines amino acid residue types,
functional-group categories, explicit physicochemical descriptors, and ESM2
residue embeddings to generate contextualized sequence states. In parallel, a
spatial-geometric subbranch constructs a hybrid residue graph containing both
sequential adjacency edges and spatial-neighborhood edges and propagates
geometric information through pLDDT-aware equivariant graph layers.

To overcome the limited chemical resolution of a C$\alpha$-only residue graph,
GHXTox additionally extracts typed chemical sites from the full-atom
structure and performs message passing using dual-scale distances, directional
relationships, interaction-compatibility channels, and structural confidence.
For peptides with available conformer ensembles, an additional statistical
residual branch summarizes residue fluctuations, distance variation, and
contact occupancy. The residual is constrained to exactly zero when conformer
information is unavailable.

Rather than discarding low-confidence structures through hard filtering,
GHXTox treats pLDDT as a continuous reliability signal and incorporates it
into geometric edge weighting, chemical-site messages, residue-level
structural gating, and peptide-level structural gating. Low-confidence
structural evidence is therefore down-weighted rather than removed, preserving
structural coverage while limiting the influence of uncertain coordinates.
pLDDT is interpreted here as a local confidence estimate produced by the
structure-prediction model, not as an experimentally determined measure of
structural accuracy, conformational flexibility, or biological activity. The
detailed architecture of the structural expert is illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:expert_detail}.

\subsubsection{Peptide Structure Prediction and Conformer Preparation}
For a peptide sequence of length $L$, $S = (a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_L)$,
ESMFold is first used to predict its three-dimensional structure
\cite{lin2023esmfold}. The predicted structure is stored as a full-atom PDB file,
from which residue-level C$\alpha$ coordinates, backbone coordinates,
full-atom-derived chemical-site coordinates, and pLDDT scores are extracted.
This operation is denoted
\begin{equation*}
    (\mathbf X, \mathcal{A}, \mathbf q) = \operatorname{ESMFold}(\mathcal S),
    \qquad \mathbf X = [\mathbf x_1, \ldots, \mathbf x_L]^{\top} \in
    \mathbb{R}^{L\times3},
    \quad \mathbf q = (q_1, \ldots, q_L) \in [0,1]^L,
\end{equation*}
where $\mathbf X$ contains the C$\alpha$ coordinates, $\mathcal{A}$ denotes
the full-atom-PDB-derived backbone and chemical-site geometry, and
$\mathbf q$ contains the residue-level pLDDT confidence scores rescaled to
$[0,1]$. The C$\alpha$ coordinates serve as the primary spatial variables of
the residue-level equivariant graph, whereas the full-atom coordinates are
used to construct local chemical sites. Before geometric encoding, the
coordinates are centered at the peptide centroid to remove the effect of
absolute translation.

A single predicted structure provides a common structural anchor but cannot
represent potential conformational heterogeneity. The conformer-residual
stage was therefore trained on a multi-source conformer variant using a
2{,}795-peptide cohort with up to eight validated, aligned
conformers per peptide. PepFlow supplies 761 training records of 11--15
residues, whereas AlphaFold2/ColabFold supplies 2{,}034 records of 16--30
residues \cite{abdin2024pepflow,jumper2021alphafold2,mirdita2022colabfold}.
These sources are represented explicitly in the residual head; nevertheless,
generator identity remains partially coupled to peptide length and is treated
as a limitation rather than as an independently identified causal factor.

For controlled ablation only, conformer statistics were admitted from a
validated cache whose source and sequence-matching policies were fixed without
using Test1 or Test2 labels. The Test1 cache contains eight conformers for each
of 486 peptides: 130 PepFlow-derived records of 3--15 residues and 356
AlphaFold2/ColabFold-derived records of 16--30 residues. Test2 reuses 284 of
these ensembles through an exact unique-sequence join. These caches are not
used by the final benchmark predictor or the public inference interface.
For every admitted ensemble, residue indices are matched to a common sequence
order. An ensemble is represented as
$\mathcal{C} = \{\mathbf X^{(1)}, \ldots, \mathbf X^{(M)}\}$, $1 \leq M \leq
8$, where $\mathbf X^{(m)} \in \mathbb{R}^{L\times3}$ denotes the C$\alpha$
coordinates of conformer $m$. Rigid-body alignment is performed before the
ensemble statistics are calculated, ensuring that the resulting variation
reflects internal geometric changes rather than arbitrary global rotations or
translations.

Because conformer ensembles are not available for every training peptide, a
binary availability indicator is defined as
\begin{equation}
    c_{\mathrm{conf}} =
    \begin{cases}
        1, & \text{if valid ensemble statistics are available,}\\
        0, & \text{otherwise.}
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:conf_available}
\end{equation}
When $c_{\mathrm{conf}} = 0$, the conformer-statistics tensors are filled
with zeros, and the corresponding residual contribution is explicitly
suppressed by the availability indicator. Missing ensemble information is
therefore not interpreted as evidence of structural rigidity; instead, the
model falls back exactly to the single-structure pathway. Under the benchmark
ablation, valid conformer statistics are available for 486/1{,}126
(43.16\%) Test1 peptides and 284/582 (48.80\%) Test2 peptides. For the main
comparison, the availability indicator is fixed to zero for every benchmark
peptide and the structural probability is read from the single-structure base
logits.

The generated conformers are computational samples rather than experimentally
determined trajectories or rigorous thermodynamic ensembles. Consequently,
RMSF, contact occupancy, and distance variance are interpreted as
sampling-derived descriptors of conformational variation rather than direct
measurements of peptide dynamics in solution.

\subsubsection{Residue Feature Construction and Hybrid Geometric Graph}
The structural expert constructs both an internal sequence representation and
a spatial-geometric representation at the residue level. The internal
sequence-semantic subbranch described here uses frozen ESM2 residue
embeddings and a lightweight Transformer. It is distinct from the
LoRA-ProtT5 and bidirectional Mamba expert described in Section 2.3. The
former supports residue-level sequence--structure alignment inside the
structural expert, whereas the latter acts as an independent top-level 1D
prediction expert.

For residue $i$, the input to the internal sequence-semantic subbranch is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf u_i^{\mathrm{seq}} = \left[\mathbf e_i^{\mathrm{AA}} \parallel
    \mathbf e_i^{\mathrm{group}} \parallel \mathbf f_i^{\mathrm{res}} \parallel
    P_{\mathrm{ESM}}\left(\mathbf e_i^{\mathrm{ESM2}}\right)\right],
    \label{eq:seq_feat}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf e_i^{\mathrm{AA}} \in \mathbb{R}^{32}$ is the amino acid
category embedding, $\mathbf e_i^{\mathrm{group}} \in \mathbb{R}^{12}$ is the
functional-group category embedding, $\mathbf f_i^{\mathrm{res}} \in
\mathbb{R}^{42}$ denotes the explicit residue-level physicochemical
descriptors, and $\mathbf e_i^{\mathrm{ESM2}} \in \mathbb{R}^{1280}$ is the
ESM2-650M residue representation; the projection $P_{\mathrm{ESM}}$ maps the
ESM2 representation to 128 dimensions. Stacking the residue-level feature
vectors yields the sequence-feature matrix $U_{\mathrm{seq}} \in
\mathbb{R}^{L \times 214}$, where $214 = 32 + 12 + 42 + 128$. After a linear
projection and positional encoding, a two-layer Transformer encoder with
hidden dimension 128, four attention heads, and a feed-forward dimension of
512 produces the contextualized sequence states $\mathbf H^{\mathrm{seq}} \in
\mathbb{R}^{L \times 128}$. This representation encodes the contextual
sequence semantics of each residue and subsequently serves as the query
representation in the sequence--structure cross-attention module.

In the spatial subbranch, the initial representation of residue $i$ is
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbf h_i^{(0)} = P_{\mathrm{sp}}\left[\mathbf f_i^{\mathrm{res}}
    \parallel \mathbf g_i^{\mathrm{geo}} \parallel q_i \parallel
    P_{\mathrm{ESM}}\left(\mathbf e_i^{\mathrm{ESM2}}\right)\right],
\end{equation*}
where $\mathbf g_i^{\mathrm{geo}} \in \mathbb{R}^{16}$ is a lightweight
geometric descriptor derived from the C$\alpha$ coordinates and pLDDT scores.
The
16-dimensional descriptor includes (i) residue-level pLDDT and local
confidence statistics; (ii) radial position, contact fractions, and soft
spatial density; (iii) nearest-neighbor, nonlocal-neighbor, and local
sequential distances; and (iv) sine/cosine encodings of the C$\alpha$-trace
angles and torsions. These explicit descriptors provide stable local
geometric priors without introducing an overly complex handcrafted
representation for short and uncertain predicted peptide structures.

GHXTox then constructs a hybrid residue graph:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{G} = \left(\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{seq}} \cup
    \mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{knn}}\right),
    \label{eq:hybrid_graph}
\end{equation}
where each residue corresponds to one node. Sequential edges
$\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{seq}}$ connect adjacent residues and preserve
peptide-chain continuity, whereas $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{knn}}$ connects each
residue to its 10 nearest spatial neighbors and is symmetrized. The hybrid
graph therefore captures both covalent sequence order and nonlocal spatial
contacts while avoiding large variations in graph density that may arise from
a single fixed-distance cutoff.
 Equivalently, $A_{ij} \in \{0,1\}$ denotes whether residues $i$ and $j$ are
connected by a sequential or k-nearest-neighbor edge in the hybrid graph.

For residues $i$ and $j$, the C$\alpha$ distance
$d_{ij} = \lVert \mathbf x_i - \mathbf x_j \rVert_2$ is encoded by 16 radial
basis functions with centers $\mu_k$ uniformly spaced over 0--24 Å,
$\phi_k(d_{ij}) = \exp[-\gamma(d_{ij}-\mu_k)^2]$ with
$\gamma = 1/(\mu_{k+1}-\mu_k)^2$. The base edge representation additionally
includes the pairwise structural confidence and the normalized sequence
separation:
\begin{equation}
    c_{ij} = \sqrt{q_i q_j}, \qquad
    s_{ij} = \frac{\log(1 + |i - j|)}{\log(L + 1)}.
    \label{eq:edge_feat}
\end{equation}
Thus, sequentially adjacent residues receive small values, whereas residues
separated by longer sequence intervals approach one. The resulting edge
feature is therefore $\mathbf e_{ij} = \left[\boldsymbol\phi(d_{ij}) \parallel
c_{ij} \parallel s_{ij}\right]$. This formulation allows the model to
distinguish sequentially adjacent contacts, long-range spatial contacts, and
contacts supported by different levels of structural confidence.

\subsubsection{pLDDT-Aware Equivariant Graph Encoding}
To learn three-dimensional representations without relying on a particular
global coordinate frame, the spatial branch employs three E(3)-equivariant
graph neural network layers \cite{satorras2021egnn}. The network updates residue features
using rotation- and translation-invariant quantities while updating
coordinates equivariantly. Consequently, a global rigid transformation of the
peptide structure does not change the predicted toxicity, whereas
intermediate coordinate representations transform consistently with the
input.

At layer $\ell$, the message from residue $j$ to residue $i$ is computed as
$\mathbf m_{ij}^{(\ell)} = \phi_e^{(\ell)}[\mathbf h_i^{(\ell)} \parallel
\mathbf h_j^{(\ell)} \parallel \mathbf e_{ij}^{(\ell)}]$, where
$\phi_e^{(\ell)}$ is a message multilayer perceptron. To prevent uncertain or
distant contacts from contributing as strongly as local high-confidence
contacts, a continuous edge weight is defined as
\begin{equation}
    w_{ij}^{(\ell)} = A_{ij}\, \exp\!\left(-\frac{d_{ij}^{(\ell)}}{12}\right)
    \left[q_{\min} + \left(1 - q_{\min}\right) c_{ij}^{\rho}\right],
    \qquad q_{\min} = 0.1, \quad \rho = 1.5,
    \label{eq:edge_weight}
\end{equation}
The term $\exp(-d_{ij}^{(\ell)}/12)$ imposes a smooth distance decay with
a characteristic decay length of 12 Å, and both the distance and the edge
weight are recomputed at each layer from the current coordinates. Here,
$A_{ij} \in \{0,1\}$ is the adjacency indicator of the hybrid graph
defined in Section 2.4.2, and $c_{ij}$ is the pairwise structural
confidence defined in Section 2.4.2. The minimum
weight $q_{\min}$ prevents low-confidence regions from being completely
disconnected, whereas the exponent $\rho$ increases the relative contribution
of high-confidence contacts. The distance-decay term further reduces the
influence of more distant spatial neighbors.

Messages received by node $i$ are aggregated with a normalization floor, and
the EGNN layer updates node features and coordinates as
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathbf m_i^{(\ell)} &= \frac{\sum_j w_{ij}^{(\ell)}\,
        \mathbf m_{ij}^{(\ell)}}
        {\max\!\left(\sum_j w_{ij}^{(\ell)},\, 1\right)},\\
        \mathbf h_i^{(\ell+1)} &= \operatorname{LN}\left\{\mathbf h_i^{(\ell)}
        + \phi_h^{(\ell)}\left[\mathbf h_i^{(\ell)} \parallel
        \mathbf m_i^{(\ell)}\right]\right\},\\
        \mathbf x_i^{(\ell+1)} &= \mathbf x_i^{(\ell)} + 0.05\,
        \frac{\sum_j \alpha_{ij}^{(\ell)}
        \left(\mathbf x_i^{(\ell)} - \mathbf x_j^{(\ell)}\right)}
        {\max\!\left(\sum_j w_{ij}^{(\ell)},\, 1\right)}.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:egnn_update}
\end{equation}
where
$\alpha_{ij}^{(\ell)} = \tanh[\phi_x^{(\ell)}(\mathbf m_{ij}^{(\ell)})]\,
w_{ij}^{(\ell)}$ is a bounded coordinate-update coefficient generated from
the edge message and pairwise relative vectors. The coordinates are recentered
after every layer to reduce numerical drift. Because the update depends only
on scalar messages, distances, and relative coordinate vectors, the operation
remains equivariant to rigid rotations and translations.

Three EGNN layers are stacked to progressively enlarge the spatial receptive
field. The first layer primarily encodes direct geometric neighbors, whereas
subsequent layers integrate higher-order spatial environments through
repeated message passing. Unlike approaches that simply append pLDDT as an
ordinary node feature, GHXTox uses pLDDT to directly modulate the strength of
geometric messages, allowing structural uncertainty to influence
representation learning at the propagation stage.

Continuous down-weighting does not imply that a low-pLDDT residue is
biologically unimportant. Low confidence may arise from prediction error or
potential conformational flexibility; the model reduces the reliability
assigned to uncertain coordinate evidence while preserving the residue's
sequence-semantic and physicochemical features.

\subsubsection{Full-Atom Chemical-Site Message Passing}
A C$\alpha$-based residue graph describes the global fold and spatial neighborhoods
but cannot distinguish chemically distinct regions within the same side
chain. For example, tyrosine contains both an aromatic ring and a hydroxyl
group, whereas tryptophan contains an aromatic system and an indole nitrogen.
Compressing each side chain into a single centroid would mix these chemically
different moieties. GHXTox therefore extracts chemistry-aware pseudo-sites
from the predicted full-atom PDB structure and processes them with a dedicated
local chemical branch.

Up to two chemical sites are kept for each residue. Site identity is
represented by an eight-dimensional multi-hot vector $\mathbf t_{ir} \in
\{0,1\}^{8}$ allowing overlapping types: positive, negative, donor, acceptor,
aromatic, hydrophobic, sulfur, and conformational, where $r$ indexes the
chemical site within residue $i$. Multiple types may be assigned to the same
site. For example, the cysteine sulfur site is labeled as donor, hydrophobic,
and sulfur-containing, whereas the proline ring is labeled as hydrophobic and
conformationally constrained. The aromatic ring and hydroxyl group of
tyrosine are represented as separate sites, as are the aromatic system and
the indole NE1 nitrogen (donor) of tryptophan.

A site coordinate is obtained from the mean position of the heavy atoms
defining the corresponding chemical moiety. Site orientation is represented by
either a bond direction or an unsigned plane normal. Because the sign of a
plane normal is arbitrary, downstream directional features use absolute
cosines, preserving rotation invariance and eliminating orientation-sign
ambiguity.

The initial representation of site $r$ in residue $i$ is
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbf z_{ir}^{(0)} = \phi_{\mathrm{site}}\left[\mathbf t_{ir}
    \parallel q_i \parallel o_{ir}^{\mathrm{valid}}\right],
\end{equation*}
where $q_i$ is the pLDDT score of the parent residue and
$o_{ir}^{\mathrm{valid}}$ indicates whether a reliable orientation could be
constructed; the input is projected into a 64-dimensional hidden space.

Chemical-site edges are established between valid sites separated by no more
than 16 Å. Here, same-residue site edges are
kept, preserving spatial relationships between chemically different
moieties in complex side chains. Each edge contains four complementary
feature families. First, the raw distance in Å is encoded using 16 radial
basis functions, $\boldsymbol\phi_{\mathrm{raw}}(d_{uv}) \in \mathbb{R}^{16}$.
Second, a peptide-size-normalized distance is constructed. The radius of
gyration of the C$\alpha$ coordinates is
\begin{equation}
    R_g = \sqrt{\frac{1}{L} \sum_{i=1}^{L}
    \left\lVert \mathbf x_i - \bar{\mathbf x} \right\rVert_2^2},
    \qquad \bar{\mathbf x} = \frac{1}{L} \sum_{i=1}^{L} \mathbf x_i,
    \qquad \bar{d}_{uv} = \frac{d_{uv}}{R_g},
    \label{eq:normalized_distance}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{\mathbf x}$ denotes the centroid of the valid C$\alpha$ coordinates, and
the normalized distance is encoded by eight radial basis functions over the
interval 0--4, $\boldsymbol\phi_{\mathrm{norm}}(\bar{d}_{uv}) \in
\mathbb{R}^{8}$. The raw representation describes the physical separation in
Å, whereas the normalized representation describes the separation relative to
the overall peptide size. Their combination allows the model to distinguish,
for example, an 8 Å contact in a compact short peptide from an 8 Å contact in
a larger peptide.

Third, four rotation-invariant orientation features are used:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf o_{uv} = \left[|\mathbf o_u \cdot \mathbf r_{uv}|,\;
    |\mathbf o_v \cdot \mathbf r_{vu}|,\; |\mathbf o_u \cdot \mathbf o_v|,\;
    o_{uv}^{\mathrm{valid}}\right],
    \label{eq:orientation}
\end{equation}
which describe the alignment of the source orientation with the intersite
direction, the alignment of the target orientation with the reverse direction,
the mutual orientation of the two sites, and orientation availability.
Fourth, six explicit compatibility channels are used: opposite-charge,
donor--acceptor, aromatic--aromatic, positive--aromatic (cation--$\pi$),
sulfur--sulfur, and hydrophobic--hydrophobic compatibility. These channels
encode candidate chemical compatibility patterns available to the model; they
do not imply that a true salt bridge, hydrogen bond, cation--$\pi$
interaction, disulfide bond, or hydrophobic core is present in the predicted
structure.

The full chemical-site edge representation is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf e_{uv}^{\mathrm{chem}} = \left[\boldsymbol\phi_{\mathrm{raw}}
    (d_{uv}) \parallel \boldsymbol\phi_{\mathrm{norm}}(\bar{d}_{uv})
    \parallel \mathbf o_{uv} \parallel \mathbf c_{uv}^{\mathrm{chem}}
    \parallel \sqrt{q_u q_v}\right].
    \label{eq:site_edge}
\end{equation}
At layer $l$, each site message is computed from the two site states and the
edge representation, confidence-weighted, and aggregated over the
neighborhood. After two message-passing layers, valid sites belonging to the
same residue are mean-pooled, and the residue-level chemical residual and the
chemical-enhanced spatial state are
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathbf m_{uv}^{(l)} &= \phi_{\mathrm{chem}}^{(l)}
        \left[\mathbf z_u^{(l)} \parallel \mathbf z_v^{(l)}
        \parallel \mathbf e_{uv}^{\mathrm{chem}}\right],\\
        \bar{\mathbf m}_v^{(l)} &= \frac{\sum_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)}
        \sqrt{q_u q_v}\, \mathbf m_{uv}^{(l)}}
        {\max\!\left(\sum_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)} \sqrt{q_u q_v},\, 1\right)},\\
        \Delta \mathbf h_i^{\mathrm{chem}} &= \mathbf W_{\mathrm{chem}}\,
        \underset{r \in \mathcal{S}_i}{\operatorname{Mean}}
        \left(\mathbf z_{ir}^{(2)}\right),\\
        \mathbf h_i^{\mathrm{sp}} &= \mathbf h_i^{\mathrm{EGNN}} +
        \Delta \mathbf h_i^{\mathrm{chem}},
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:chem_residual}
\end{equation}
where the projection $\mathbf W_{\mathrm{chem}}$ and its bias
$\mathbf b_{\mathrm{chem}}$ are zero-initialized, so that
$\Delta \mathbf h_i^{\mathrm{chem}} = 0$ at the beginning of training. This
conservative residual design allows the new chemical-site branch to
progressively learn useful corrections from an intact geometric backbone
rather than perturbing the existing spatial representation with a randomly
initialized contribution.

\subsubsection{Availability-Masked Conformer-Ensemble Residual}
A single predicted structure provides a deterministic spatial anchor, whereas
a peptide may adopt multiple related conformations. Running the complete
geometric network independently on every conformer would be computationally
expensive and could allow the model to exploit differences between conformer
generators or uneven ensemble coverage. The current GHXTox model therefore
uses a lightweight conformer-statistics residual. The single-structure expert
remains the base predictor, and ensemble statistics are permitted to correct
its logit only when valid conformer information is available.

For a peptide with $M$ aligned conformers, we compute the residue-level
root-mean-square fluctuation, the per-pair mean distance and distance standard
deviation, and the contact occupancy at cutoff $r \in \{6, 8, 10\}$ Å:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathrm{RMSF}_i &= \sqrt{\frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M}
        \left\lVert \mathbf x_i^{(m)} - \bar{\mathbf x}_i \right\rVert_2^2}, &
        \bar{d}_{ij} &= \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} d_{ij}^{(m)},\\
        \sigma_{ij}^{d} &= \sqrt{\frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M}
        \left(d_{ij}^{(m)} - \bar{d}_{ij}\right)^2}, &
        O_{ij}^{(r)} &= \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M}
        \mathbb{I}\left(d_{ij}^{(m)} < r\right),
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:conformer_stats}
\end{equation}
with $\bar{\mathbf x}_i$ the mean conformer coordinate of residue $i$. The
8 Å contact-occupancy uncertainty
$U_{ij}^{(8)} = O_{ij}^{(8)}(1-O_{ij}^{(8)})$ is also used: values close to
zero or one indicate contacts that are consistently absent or present across
the sampled conformers, whereas values near 0.5 indicate contact switching.

The ensemble statistics are organized into an eight-dimensional residue-level
descriptor $\mathbf r_i^{\mathrm{conf}} \in \mathbb{R}^{8}$, containing the
residue RMSF; the mean pairwise distance to other residues; the standard
deviation of the mean pairwise distances associated with the residue; the
mean pairwise-distance standard deviation; the mean contact occupancies at 6,
8, and 10 Å; and the mean 8 Å contact-occupancy uncertainty, which together
form the eight-dimensional descriptor. A twelve-dimensional peptide-level
descriptor $\mathbf g^{\mathrm{conf}} \in \mathbb{R}^{12}$ is also
constructed, containing the normalized number of conformers; the mean,
standard deviation, and maximum of RMSF; the mean, standard deviation, and
maximum of the conformer RMSD; the mean and standard deviation of the radius
of gyration; the mean and maximum of the pair-distance standard deviation;
and the global mean of $U_{ij}^{(8)}$, which together form the
twelve-dimensional descriptor. Before neural encoding, distance- and
fluctuation-based statistics are rescaled by fixed constants to place
heterogeneous descriptors on comparable numerical ranges; the exact scaling
factors are reported in the implementation-details table.

The residue-level ensemble features are encoded and aggregated using both
masked mean and masked maximum pooling, whereas the global descriptor is
processed by a separate encoder, giving the ensemble embeddings
$\mathbf h_{\mathrm{conf}}^{\mathrm{res}}$ and
$\mathbf h_{\mathrm{conf}}^{\mathrm{global}}$.
The generator is encoded by a three-dimensional one-hot vector
$\mathbf s_{\mathrm{conf}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$ for PepFlow,
AlphaFold2/ColabFold, or another admitted generator. A learned source
embedding $\phi_s(\mathbf s_{\mathrm{conf}})$ is concatenated with the base
structural embedding and the two ensemble embeddings to produce a raw logit
residual
\begin{equation*}
    \Delta z_{\mathrm{conf}}^{\mathrm{raw}} = \phi_{\mathrm{conf}}\left[
    \mathbf h_{\mathrm{base}} \parallel \mathbf h_{\mathrm{conf}}^{\mathrm{res}}
    \parallel \mathbf h_{\mathrm{conf}}^{\mathrm{global}}
    \parallel \phi_s(\mathbf s_{\mathrm{conf}})\right],
\end{equation*}
whose final linear layer is initialized to zero. Using the availability
indicator $c_{\mathrm{conf}}$ defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:conf_available}, the
effective residual and the final structural-expert logit are
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \lambda_{\mathrm{conf}} &= \sigma\left(\beta_{\mathrm{conf}}\right),\\
        \Delta z_{\mathrm{conf}} &= c_{\mathrm{conf}}\, \lambda_{\mathrm{conf}}\,
        \Delta z_{\mathrm{conf}}^{\mathrm{raw}},\\
        z_{\mathrm{SE3D}} &= z_{\mathrm{base}} + \Delta z_{\mathrm{conf}},
        \qquad c_{\mathrm{conf}} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; z_{\mathrm{SE3D}} = z_{\mathrm{base}}.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:effective_residual}
\end{equation}
where the effective conformer weight is initialized to 0.25 at the beginning
of training through $\beta_{\mathrm{conf}}^{(0)} = \mathrm{logit}(0.25)$, so
that $\sigma(\beta_{\mathrm{conf}}^{(0)}) = 0.25$. Zero-filled feature tensors
alone would not guarantee a zero correction because the residual network may
contain nonzero biases; the additional hard availability mask ensures an
exact fallback to the single-structure prediction. The conformer branch is
permitted only to refine the single-structure predictor rather than replace
the geometric backbone, reducing the risk of overfitting to limited and
uneven ensemble coverage.

\subsubsection{pLDDT-Aware Sequence--Structure Cross-Attention}
After the EGNN and the chemical-site residual are applied, the model obtains
the chemical-enhanced spatial representation
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbf H^{\mathrm{sp}} = \left[\mathbf h_1^{\mathrm{sp}}, \ldots,
    \mathbf h_L^{\mathrm{sp}}\right]^{\top} \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times 128},
\end{equation*}
where $\mathbf h_i^{\mathrm{sp}}$ is defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:chem_residual}.
Spatial information alone does not determine whether a structural pattern is
relevant to the current sequence context. For example, similar hydrophobic
contacts may have different implications depending on residue identities and
sequence positions. GHXTox therefore uses pLDDT-aware cross-attention within
the structural expert, allowing contextualized sequence states to selectively
retrieve spatial evidence.

A node-level gate is first constructed for each residue from the
42-dimensional residue descriptors and the pLDDT score:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        g_i^{\mathrm{base}} &= \phi_{\mathrm{node}}\left[\mathbf f_i^{\mathrm{res}} \parallel q_i\right],\\
        \mathbf c_i^{\mathrm{node}} &= \left[\frac{q_i - \mu_q}{T_q},\; \frac{(q_i - \mu_q)^2}{T_q^2},\; \mathbb{I}(q_i < 0.55)\right],\\
        r_i^{\mathrm{node}} &= 0.5 + \sigma\left(\mathbf w_n^{\top}
        \mathbf c_i^{\mathrm{node}} + b_n\right),\\
        g_i^{\mathrm{node}} &= \mathrm{clamp}\left(g_i^{\mathrm{base}}\,
        r_i^{\mathrm{node}},\; 0, 1\right),
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:node_gate}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_q = 0.70$, $T_q = 0.12$, and the residual confidence vector
$\mathbf c_i^{\mathrm{node}}$ increases the sensitivity of the gate to the
distribution of structural confidence. The spatial representation is then
continuously down-weighted as
$\widehat{\mathbf H}^{\mathrm{sp}} = \mathbf H^{\mathrm{sp}} \odot
\mathbf g^{\mathrm{node}}$, with the node-level gate vector
$\mathbf g^{\mathrm{node}} \in [0,1]^L$ broadcast across the feature
dimension.

In the cross-attention operation, the contextualized sequence representation
serves as the query, and the gated spatial representation serves as the key
and value:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf H^{\mathrm{cross}} = \mathrm{MHA}\left(Q = \mathbf H^{\mathrm{seq}},
    K = \widehat{\mathbf H}^{\mathrm{sp}},
    V = \widehat{\mathbf H}^{\mathrm{sp}}\right).
    \label{eq:cross_attn_sp}
\end{equation}
This direction has a direct interpretation: each residue position retrieves
spatial evidence according to its contextual sequence representation. The
operation is not a direct feature interaction between the LoRA-ProtT5--Mamba
expert and the structural expert; it occurs internally between the
ESM2-enhanced sequence states and the geometric states of the structural
expert.

In addition to residue-level gating, a peptide-level gate is generated from
the overall structure-confidence profile. The model first summarizes the
profile by the mean pLDDT $\bar{q}$, the minimum pLDDT $q_{\min}$, and the
low-confidence fraction
$r_{\mathrm{low}} = \frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathbb{I}(q_i < 0.55)$, and
constructs the peptide-level confidence vector
$\mathbf c^{\mathrm{pep}} = \left[(\bar{q} - \mu_q)/T_q,\;
(q_{\min} - \mu_q)/T_q,\; r_{\mathrm{low}}\right]$. The base peptide-level
gate and its residual-adjusted form are
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        g_{\mathrm{pep}}^{\mathrm{base}} &= \phi_{\mathrm{global}}\left[\bar{q},\; q_{\min},\; r_{\mathrm{low}}\right],\\
        r_{\mathrm{pep}} &= 0.5 + \sigma\left(\mathbf w_{\mathrm{pep}}^{\top} \mathbf c^{\mathrm{pep}} + b_{\mathrm{pep}}\right),\\
        g_{\mathrm{pep}} &= \mathrm{clamp}\left(g_{\mathrm{pep}}^{\mathrm{base}}\, r_{\mathrm{pep}},\; 0, 1\right),
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:peptide_gate}
\end{equation}
where the peptide-level residual scale is analogous to, but separately
parameterized from, the node-level residual scale, and $g_{\mathrm{pep}} \in
[0,1]$ denotes the scalar peptide-level structural gate.

The final residue-level fusion representation combines the sequence state
with the gated structural context,
$\mathbf H^{\mathrm{fused}} = \operatorname{LN}\left[\mathbf H^{\mathrm{seq}}
\parallel g_{\mathrm{pep}}\, \mathbf H^{\mathrm{cross}}\right]$.
The two gating levels answer complementary questions: how reliable the
structural evidence is at an individual residue position, and how strongly the
structural branch should be trusted for the peptide as a whole. The sequence
representation is always kept, whereas the structural context is
dynamically scaled according to prediction confidence. Consequently, when the
predicted structure has low overall confidence, the structural expert can
still rely on its internal sequence-semantic pathway without allowing
uncertain coordinates to dominate the classification.

\subsubsection{Global Feature Integration and Structural Classification}
After pLDDT-aware cross-attention, each valid residue is represented by a
256-dimensional fused vector obtained by concatenating the 128-dimensional
internal sequence state with the 128-dimensional structural context. Masked
mean pooling is applied over the valid residue positions, and the pooled
vector is concatenated with five global physicochemical descriptors before a
three-layer MLP produces the base structural logit:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathbf h_{\mathrm{pool}} &= \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{L} m_i\,
        \mathbf H^{\mathrm{fused}}_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{L} m_i} \in \mathbb{R}^{256},\\
        \mathbf h_{\mathrm{cls}} &= \left[\mathbf h_{\mathrm{pool}} \parallel
        \mathbf f_{\mathrm{pep}}^{\mathrm{phys}}\right] \in \mathbb{R}^{261},\\
        z_{\mathrm{base}} &= \phi_{\mathrm{cls}}\left(\mathbf h_{\mathrm{cls}}\right),
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:final_3d_repr}
\end{equation}
where the mask excludes padding positions from the pooled representation, and
$\mathbf f_{\mathrm{pep}}^{\mathrm{phys}} \in \mathbb{R}^{5}$ contains the
normalized peptide length, the length-normalized net charge, the
aromatic-residue fraction, the cysteine fraction, and the scaled mean
hydropathy. $\phi_{\mathrm{cls}}$ is a three-layer MLP with dimensions
$261 \rightarrow 128 \rightarrow 64 \rightarrow 1$, each hidden layer applying
GELU activation and dropout. Unlike maximum pooling, masked mean pooling
integrates structural evidence distributed across residue positions, which is
appropriate when toxicity-related spatial patterns are not confined to a
single local region.

Applying the availability-masked conformer correction defined in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:effective_residual} yields the final structural-expert logit,
and the structural-expert probability is
\[
    z_{\mathrm{SE3D}} = z_{\mathrm{base}} + c_{\mathrm{conf}}\,
    \lambda_{\mathrm{conf}}\, \Delta z_{\mathrm{conf}}^{\mathrm{raw}},
    \qquad p_{\mathrm{SE3D}} = \sigma\left(z_{\mathrm{SE3D}}\right),
\]
as defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:effective_residual}. The resulting probability
is passed directly to the dual-expert fusion in Section 2.2; the standalone
structural threshold is used only for branch-specific analyses.

\subsection{Training and Model-Selection Protocol}
The two experts were trained independently because they differ in input
representation, parameter scale, and optimization behavior, and they are
combined only at the probability level. Group-aware training out-of-fold
(OOF) predictions were used for calibration, fusion-weight selection, and
threshold determination, and Test1/Test2 labels were excluded from all of
these procedures. Highly similar peptides are confined to the same fold
(Section 2.5.1); because both test sets were observed during development,
they serve as frozen retrospective benchmarks rather than fresh external
validation.

\subsubsection{Group-Aware Five-Fold OOF Generation}
The training set $\mathcal{D} = \{(\mathcal{S}_i, y_i, g_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$,
$N = 6387$, consists of peptide sequences $\mathcal{S}_i$, toxicity labels
$y_i \in \{0,1\}$, and sequence-homology groups $g_i$. A fixed five-fold
manifest was constructed by CD-HIT clustering of the training sequences at a
sequence-identity threshold of 0.80; this fold-level grouping is distinct
from the 0.9 redundancy-removal threshold used for dataset construction in
Section 2.1. The data are partitioned into five disjoint validation folds:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathcal{D} &= \bigcup_{k=1}^{5}\mathcal{D}_k, &
        \mathcal{D}_k \cap \mathcal{D}_l &= \varnothing, \quad k \neq l,\\
        g_i = g_j &\Longrightarrow f(i) = f(j),
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:folds}
\end{equation}
where $f(i)$ denotes the fold assigned to sample $i$; all peptides in the
same homology group therefore remain in the same fold. For fold $k$, the
model is trained only on the complement $\mathcal{D}_{-k} = \mathcal{D}
\setminus \mathcal{D}_k$ and evaluated on the unseen fold $\mathcal{D}_k$.
The OOF probability of sample $i$ is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{D}_{-k} = \mathcal{D} \setminus \mathcal{D}_k, \qquad
    \widehat{p}_i^{\mathrm{OOF}} = F_{\boldsymbol\theta^{(-k)}}\left(
    \mathcal{S}_i\right), \quad i \in \mathcal{D}_k,
    \label{eq:oof_prob}
\end{equation}
so each training peptide receives exactly one probability from a model that
did not observe it during optimization.

For the 1D expert, seed-specific OOF probability vectors were aligned and
averaged before calibration (Section 2.3.4). The structural expert uses the
same folds; each fold-specific checkpoint produces OOF probabilities for its
held-out fold, with conformer statistics handled by the availability mask
defined in Section 2.4.5. Parameters selected from the complete OOF table
remain development-stage choices and are evaluated on independent test sets.

\subsubsection{Loss Functions and Class-Imbalance Handling}
The 1D expert uses the positive-class-weighted binary cross-entropy defined
in Section 2.3.3, with class counts recomputed from each fold's training
partition; no synthetic or point-mutated samples were used.

The structural expert is trained in stages. In the base geometric and
chemical-site stage, the full-atom chemical-site branch is initialized from a
trained pLDDT-aware geometric model while the remaining backbone is frozen,
using the same weighted BCE. During this stage, pLDDT-dependent Gaussian
coordinate perturbations are applied:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_i = \sigma_0\left[1 + \kappa\left(1 - q_i\right)\right],
    \qquad
    \boldsymbol\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf 0,
    \sigma_i^2 \mathbf I_3\right), \qquad
    \widetilde{\mathbf x}_i = \mathbf x_i + \boldsymbol\varepsilon_i,
    \label{eq:perturb}
\end{equation}
with $\sigma_0 = 0.12\ \text{Å}$ and $\kappa = 2.0$. The same displacement is
applied to the backbone, functional-group, and chemical-site coordinates so
that intra-residue geometry is preserved, and low-pLDDT residues receive
stronger perturbations than high-confidence residues; no perturbation is used
during validation or inference.

In the conformer-ensemble residual stage, the pLDDT-aware geometric backbone,
internal sequence encoder, chemical-site branch, and base classifier are
frozen, and only the conformer encoders and residual MLP are optimized. This
stage uses unweighted BCE with a residual-magnitude penalty:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{conf}} = \operatorname{BCEWithLogits}\left(z_{\mathrm{SE3D}},
    y\right) + \lambda_\Delta\,
    \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{B} c_i \left|\Delta z_{\mathrm{conf},i}\right|}
    {\max\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{B} c_i,\, 1\right)},
    \qquad \lambda_\Delta = 0.005,
    \label{eq:loss_conf}
\end{equation}
where the penalty is computed only on samples with available conformer
information. The residual layer is zero-initialized and gated by the hard
conformer-availability mask, so the initial model is equivalent to the
single-structure expert and uncovered samples receive exactly zero
correction.

\subsubsection{OOF-Based Calibration, Fusion-Weight Selection, and Threshold Freezing}
After the expert-level OOF predictions were generated, model selection
proceeded at the probability level: no network parameters were updated, and
only training OOF probabilities were used. The three seed-specific 1D OOF
vectors were averaged and calibrated with the monotonic Platt mapping defined
in Section 2.3.4; the structural-expert probability was used directly without
an additional top-level calibrator. The calibrated 1D probability and the
structural probability were then combined as in Section 2.2, where
$p_{i,\mathrm{SE3D}}^{\mathrm{OOF}}$ is the pure structure probability and
contains no contribution from the 1D predictor. No stacking classifier or
sample-specific routing network was fitted.

The fusion weight $w$ and decision threshold $\tau$ were selected by
maximizing MCC on the complete training OOF table, with BACC as the secondary
criterion for ties:
\begin{equation}
    (w^*, \tau^*) = \arg\max_{w,\tau}\;
    \mathrm{MCC}\left(\mathbf y, \hat{\mathbf y}(w, \tau)\right).
    \label{eq:selection}
\end{equation}
This yielded $w_{\mathrm{1D}}^* = 0.72$, $w_{\mathrm{SE3D}}^* = 0.28$, and
$\tau^* = 0.47022256531284556$ (0.4702 in tables). The fused probability and
class prediction follow the rules in Sections 2.2 and 2.6. A
leave-one-fold-out check indicated that the selected parameters were not
disproportionately dependent on any single fold. These values were then
frozen and applied unchanged to Test1 and Test2; benchmark labels were not
used for recalibration or parameter adjustment.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
Because the benchmark data are class-imbalanced (Test2 contains 46 toxic and
536 non-toxic peptides), MCC and BACC were used as the primary
threshold-dependent metrics, whereas ACC, sensitivity (SN), specificity (SP),
precision, and F1 were reported for completeness. AUROC and AUPRC quantified
ranking performance, and the Brier score and ECE-10 assessed probability
quality. All threshold-dependent metrics were computed at the fixed decision
threshold $\tau^* = 0.47022256531284556$ (rounded to 0.4702 in tables),
determined from the complete training OOF table (Section 2.5.4) and applied
unchanged to Test1 and Test2; ranking and calibration metrics were computed
from the continuous probabilities $p_i$.

At the fixed threshold $\tau$, binary predictions are
$\hat{y}_i = \mathbb{I}[p_i \geq \tau]$ for $N$ peptides with labels
$y_i \in \{0,1\}$ and predicted probabilities $p_i$; they are summarized by
true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false positives (FP), and false
negatives (FN), with
$\mathrm{ACC}=(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{TN})/N$,
$\mathrm{SN}=\mathrm{TP}/(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN})$,
$\mathrm{SP}=\mathrm{TN}/(\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP})$,
$F_1=2\mathrm{TP}/(2\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{FN})$, and
$\mathrm{BACC}=(\mathrm{SN}+\mathrm{SP})/2$.

\textbf{Matthews correlation coefficient.}
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{MCC} = \frac{\mathrm{TP} \cdot \mathrm{TN} -
    \mathrm{FP} \cdot \mathrm{FN}}
    {\sqrt{(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP})(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN})
    (\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP})(\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FN})}},
    \label{eq:mcc}
\end{equation}
with $-1 \leq \mathrm{MCC} \leq 1$. Because MCC jointly accounts for all four
counts, it was used as the primary fixed-threshold summary metric and as the
objective for OOF fusion-parameter selection \cite{matthews1975mcc}.

\textbf{Brier score and ranking metrics.}
AUROC is the probability that a randomly selected toxic peptide is ranked
above a randomly selected non-toxic peptide; AUPRC was computed as average
precision, whose chance-level baseline depends on positive-class prevalence.
The Brier score is the mean squared difference between the predicted
probability and the binary ground-truth label \cite{brier1950}:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Brier} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N}
    \left(p_i - y_i\right)^2.
    \label{eq:brier}
\end{equation}
It ranges from 0 to 1 and penalizes both incorrect predictions and excessive
confidence.

\textbf{Ten-bin expected calibration error.}
ECE-10 divides predictions into ten equal-width bins over $[0,1]$, with the
final bin including $p_i = 1$. Let $\mathcal{B}_m$ denote the $m$-th bin,
with mean confidence $\mathrm{conf}(\mathcal{B}_m)$ and observed
toxic-peptide frequency $\mathrm{freq}(\mathcal{B}_m)$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{ECE}_{10} = \sum_{m=1}^{10} \frac{|\mathcal{B}_m|}{N}\;
    \left|\mathrm{conf}\left(\mathcal{B}_m\right) -
    \mathrm{freq}\left(\mathcal{B}_m\right)\right|.
    \label{eq:ece}
\end{equation}
Values closer to zero indicate better agreement between predicted confidence
and observed toxicity frequency. Because ECE depends on the binning scheme,
the number and boundaries of the bins remain fixed across models and
datasets.

\subsection{Robustness, Uncertainty, and Statistical Analysis}
A single point estimate cannot characterize the reliability of a deep
predictor: performance varies with random initialization and with the finite
evaluation sample, and aggregate metrics may conceal heterogeneity across
subgroups. All analyses below were therefore performed on the frozen
predictor, without retraining, recalibration, or threshold adjustment in
response to their results. Detailed resampling procedures, seed-level
results, and the training-OOF conformer information-ceiling diagnostics are
reported in the Supporting Information.

\subsubsection{Multi-Seed Reproducibility and Prediction Dispersion}
To assess sensitivity to stochastic optimization, three full-data 1D models
were trained with seeds 42, 123, and 2025 under identical data partitions,
architecture, training duration, and hyperparameters. The 1D expert is the
equal-weight probability average of the three calibrated models rather than
the best-performing seed selected post hoc; a multi-checkpoint 3D ensemble
was evaluated but did not satisfy the prespecified improvement criterion and
was not adopted. For an evaluation metric $M$, seed-specific results may be
summarized as $\bar{M} \pm \mathrm{SD}(M)$ across the three seeds, always
with the same threshold.

Prediction dispersion is additionally quantified at the sample level:
$u_{i,\mathrm{seed}}$ is the standard deviation of the three uncalibrated seed
probabilities for peptide $i$. A large value flags predictions that are
sensitive to optimization randomness and may mark boundary or
expert-disagreement samples, but it is an empirical training-variability
measure, not a Bayesian uncertainty estimate. Because the calibrated ensemble
is a deterministic function of the three trained models, seed-level
variability is summarized through $u_{i,\mathrm{seed}}$ rather than assigned
to the averaged prediction.

\subsubsection{Class-Stratified Bootstrap Confidence Intervals}
Finite test sets are themselves samples, and this matters especially for
Test2, where a small number of positive-class changes can substantially alter
SN, BACC, and MCC. We therefore report 95\% percentile confidence intervals
from class-stratified bootstrap resampling that preserves the original size
of each class \cite{efron1993bootstrap}; the interval is formed from the
empirical 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the replicate metric values. The
primary test-set analysis used $B=5000$ replicates, and subgroup-difference
analyses used 1000. Within every replicate, threshold-dependent metrics
retain the fixed decision threshold, which is never reoptimized on resampled
data; AUROC, AUPRC, and the Brier score are recomputed from the resampled
continuous probabilities. These intervals quantify finite-sample uncertainty
conditional on the frozen predictor and the observed evaluation distribution;
they do not incorporate training-seed variability or distribution shift and
are therefore interpreted separately from the multi-seed and subgroup
analyses.

\subsubsection{Paired Bootstrap and Randomization Significance Testing}
Comparisons between the current model and an internal baseline are paired
because both models score the same peptides. We therefore analyze the metric
difference $\Delta_M^{\mathrm{obs}} = M_B - M_A$ directly on aligned samples
rather than comparing independent intervals. A paired class-stratified
bootstrap applies the same resampled indices to both models, and its 95\%
percentile interval characterizes the difference; a within-sample exchange
randomization test additionally evaluates whether the two model outputs are
exchangeable for each peptide \cite{ojala2010permutation}. Both procedures
used 5{,}000 replicates with a fixed random seed. For higher-is-better
metrics, an interval entirely above zero supports a consistent positive
difference; for lower-is-better measures such as the Brier score, improvement
corresponds to an interval entirely below zero.

MCC and BACC were the prespecified primary summary measures; SN, SP, AUROC,
AUPRC, and the Brier score provided complementary evidence, and their
$p$-values are interpreted descriptively because several correlated metrics
are examined together. Finally, because Test1 and Test2 were observed during
historical development, paired intervals and randomization tests on these
sets provide retrospective evidence for model differences rather than
prospective confirmation. Detailed exchange mechanics and the randomization
$p$-value calculation are given in the Supporting Information.

\subsubsection{Subgroup and Structure-Quality Analysis}
Aggregate metrics may conceal heterogeneity across peptide lengths,
physicochemical properties, and structure-quality ranges. We therefore
conducted prespecified subgroup analyses without modifying the model or
threshold. Length strata were 11--15, 16--30, and 31--50 residues; structural
diagnostics additionally stratified by mean pLDDT (<0.55, 0.55--0.70,
$\geq$0.70), net charge, mean hydropathy, cysteine count, and chemical-site
coverage (<0.80, 0.80--0.95, $\geq$0.95), where site coverage is the fraction
of residues containing at least one valid chemical site. A stratum was
reported only when it contained at least 20 peptides and at least five from
each class; ineligible strata were recorded but excluded from metric
interpretation.

All subgroup metrics used the global threshold and the original continuous
probabilities, with no stratum-specific threshold or model fitting. When the
current model was compared with a baseline, the subgroup difference
$\Delta M_g = M_{B,g} - M_{A,g}$ was accompanied by 1{,}000-replicate
class-stratified bootstrap intervals for MCC and AUPRC. Because many variables
and bins are examined, subgroup findings were treated as heterogeneity
diagnostics and interpreted together with stratum sizes, neighboring-stratum
trends, and interval overlap rather than as independent discoveries. pLDDT is
a prediction-confidence estimate rather than experimental structural
accuracy, so high-pLDDT performance does not by itself establish that the
corresponding structures are experimentally correct.

\subsubsection{Selective Prediction with an OOF-Derived Cutoff}
Selective prediction permits abstention on high-uncertainty peptides while
leaving the checkpoints, calibrated probabilities, fusion weights, binary
threshold, and binary predictions unchanged. No auxiliary error model was
fitted: for peptide $i$, uncertainty is the normalized proximity of the fused
probability $p_i$ to the fixed threshold $\tau$:
\begin{equation}
    d_i = 1 - \frac{\left|p_i-\tau\right|}
    {\max(\tau,1-\tau)},
    \qquad \tau=0.47022256531284556.
    \label{eq:selective_uncertainty}
\end{equation}
A larger $d_i$ indicates a prediction closer to the decision boundary and is
therefore treated as less reliable. Expert disagreement, seed dispersion,
pLDDT, and conformer-derived variables were not included in the formal policy.

The abstention cutoff was determined exclusively from group-aware training
OOF predictions. A prespecified target coverage of 90\% produced
$d_{\mathrm{OOF}}^{*}=0.47361765874159856$, after which the rule was fixed:
\begin{equation}
    d_i \leq d_{\mathrm{OOF}}^{*} \Rightarrow \mathrm{Accept},
    \qquad
    d_i > d_{\mathrm{OOF}}^{*} \Rightarrow \mathrm{Abstain}.
    \label{eq:selective_decision}
\end{equation}
The margin-only score was selected from prespecified candidate uncertainty
representations using training-OOF risk--coverage performance; once selected,
its score computation and the benchmark accept/abstain decisions require
neither class labels nor benchmark identity. No benchmark-specific quantile or
target coverage was recomputed during transfer.

Risk--coverage behavior was summarized at OOF coverages of 100\%, 95\%, 90\%,
85\%, and 80\% using accepted-sample error, MCC, BACC, sensitivity, and
specificity. Equal-count random rejection, evaluated with 5{,}000 random masks
that retained exactly the same number of observations as the frozen rule,
served as the control, and paired class-stratified bootstrap intervals were
calculated from 5{,}000 replicates while the frozen acceptance mask was
indexed directly onto each resampled set.

\subsection{Interpretability Analysis Protocol}
Interpretability analyses are defined on the model and do not
participate in feature selection, threshold selection, or fusion-weight
optimization. Unless otherwise stated, probability changes are computed with
all non-target inputs held fixed, summarized separately for toxic and
non-toxic peptides, and accompanied by peptide-level bootstrap intervals.
The analyses therefore describe model sensitivity and expert complementarity;
they are not interpreted as evidence of a causal toxicophore.

\subsubsection{Fixed-Representation Residue Occlusion and Seed Stability}
Residue-level sensitivity was evaluated on a prespecified 16-peptide cohort
selected before inspection of the attribution values: within each test set
and each expert-outcome group, one toxic and one non-toxic peptide nearest
the fusion threshold were chosen, with ties resolved by source index. For
each of the three 1D checkpoints, one contextual residue representation was
zeroed while all other representations and model parameters were kept fixed.
The per-seed occlusion effect was the unoccluded minus occluded logit; the
three raw seed probabilities were averaged before the frozen OOF-fitted Platt
transform, and the displayed attribution was the calibrated probability
change normalized by the within-peptide $L_1$ norm. Stability was summarized
by residue-wise sign agreement and pairwise Spearman correlation of absolute
effects across seeds. This experiment quantifies sensitivity to contextual
residue vectors; it is not a token-mutation or Integrated Gradients analysis
and does not test biological causality.

\subsubsection{Chemical-Site Deletion Analysis}
In an earlier secondary sensitivity analysis performed before test-time
conformer activation was discontinued, each valid modeled chemical site was
ablated
by setting its availability mask, type vector, coordinate, orientation, and
orientation-validity entry to zero. All non-target sequence features, residue
geometry, pLDDT values, other chemical sites, conformer descriptors, model
weights, and operating thresholds were held fixed. The complete frozen 3D
forward pass was recomputed, including the conformer residual when a cache
entry was available. Complementary perturbations deleted all sites
carrying one chemical category or all site slots assigned to one residue. We
report $\Delta p_{3D}=p_{3D}^{\mathrm{base}}-p_{3D}^{\mathrm{deleted}}$ and,
with the 1D probability fixed, the induced fusion change
$\Delta p_{\mathrm{fused}}=0.28\Delta p_{3D}$. Because the modeled sites and
candidate spatial relations are derived from predicted structures, these
quantities describe model sensitivity rather than confirmed chemical
bonds or intervention effects. They are not used to claim an attribution gain
for the final single-structure benchmark predictor.

\subsubsection{pLDDT--Gate Association Analysis}
The unperturbed structural expert was additionally run on all 1{,}126 Test1
and 582 Test2 peptides. For each peptide, we recorded the mean pLDDT, learned
global gate, and the within-peptide Spearman correlation between residue-level
pLDDT and node gates. Associations were summarized separately by test set and
for the pooled descriptive sample. Ninety-five percent bootstrap percentile
intervals were obtained from 2{,}000 peptide-level resamples with seed 2026.
This analysis characterizes the association between predicted-structure
confidence and learned gate values; it does not establish structural accuracy,
feature attribution, or biological causality.

\subsubsection{Expert Agreement and Conflict Analysis}
To characterize expert complementarity, each Test1 and Test2 peptide was
assigned to one of four mutually exclusive groups: both experts correct, only
the 1D expert correct, only the 3D expert correct, or both incorrect.
Standalone outcomes used the training-derived thresholds of 0.392 (1D) and
0.677819 (3D), whereas the fused predictor retained its separately fixed
threshold. Using 1D errors as the reference, we computed the fraction rescued
by the 3D expert overall and separately for toxic and non-toxic peptides, and
compared the discordant groups with respect to peptide length, cysteine
count, mean pLDDT, and chemical-site coverage. Group differences were
summarized by Cliff's delta with 5{,}000 label-stratified bootstrap
replicates and permutation tests with 10{,}000 replicates, with one
Benjamini--Hochberg correction applied across the prespecified comparison
family. Test1 and Test2 were analyzed separately. All analyses were performed
on fixed per-sample predictions.

\section{RESULTS AND DISCUSSION}
\subsection{Overall Predictive Performance on the Benchmark Test Sets}
Using the OOF-frozen weight and threshold without dataset-specific retuning,
GHXTox correctly classified 1{,}067 of 1{,}126 Test1 peptides
(TP/TN/FP/FN = 287/780/26/33). This corresponds to ACC 0.9476, BACC 0.9323,
SN 0.8969, SP 0.9677, F1 0.9068, MCC 0.8705, AUROC 0.9811, and AUPRC
0.9674. On the strongly imbalanced Test2 set, GHXTox correctly classified 551
of 582 peptides (35/516/20/11), yielding ACC 0.9467, BACC 0.8618, SN 0.7609,
SP 0.9627, F1 0.6931, MCC 0.6673, AUROC 0.9433, and AUPRC 0.8046. The lower
Test2 BACC and F1, despite high ACC, reflect the difficulty of recovering the
46 toxic peptides and demonstrate why class-balanced and precision--recall
metrics are essential for this benchmark.

Table~\ref{tab:overall_comparison} compares models reported on the same

\subsubsection{Expert Agreement, Disagreement, and Error Correction}
The experts agreed on most benchmark peptides, but the discordant samples
revealed a reproducible pattern of complementary error correction. On Test1,
the four outcome groups contained 1{,}032 both-correct, 29 1D-only-correct, 24
3D-only-correct, and 41 both-wrong peptides. The corresponding Test2 counts
were 529, 13, 15, and 25. Among the 65 Test1 peptides misclassified by the 1D
expert, the 3D expert correctly classified 24, giving a rescue rate of 36.92\%
(95\% Wilson CI, 26.23--49.08\%). It rescued 15 of 40 1D errors on Test2,
corresponding to 37.50\% (24.22--52.97\%). The structural pathway therefore
did not merely reproduce the sequence expert's decisions; it supplied correct
counter-evidence for more than one third of its errors on each benchmark.

The rescue pattern was strongly class-asymmetric. Nineteen of the 24
3D-only-correct Test1 peptides and 14 of the 15 corresponding Test2 peptides
were non-toxic. Equivalently, the 3D expert rescued 19 of 37 non-toxic 1D
errors on Test1 and 14 of 29 on Test2, compared with 5 of 28 and 1 of 11 toxic
1D errors, respectively. The structural expert mainly corrected
false-positive predictions from the sequence expert, whereas improvements in
toxic-peptide recall were limited.

Peptides classified correctly only by the 3D expert also tended to be shorter.
Their median lengths were 20 versus 25 residues on Test1 and 18 versus 28 on
Test2 when compared with the 1D-only-correct groups. The corresponding Cliff's
deltas were $-0.286$ and $-0.523$. However, none of the ten prespecified
split-by-attribute comparisons remained significant after
Benjamini--Hochberg correction; the smallest adjusted value was $q=0.156$ for
Test2 peptide length. The length pattern is therefore descriptive rather than
evidence that the structural expert is generally superior for short peptides.
Cysteine count, mean pLDDT, and chemical-site coverage likewise showed no
stable cross-benchmark separation. Conformer availability was not assigned an
independent interpretation because of its dependence on length and generator
coverage.

These results support a complementary rather than substitutive role for the
structural expert. Figure~\ref{fig:expert_complementarity} summarizes the
outcome composition, conditional rescue rates, and prespecified
discordant-group contrasts.

\subsection{Analysis of the Task-Adaptive 1D Sequence Expert}
To investigate the role of the task-adaptive 1D expert, we first compared the
current sequence architecture with the earlier sequence pipeline based on
pooled ProtT5 representations and a TextCNN classifier
\cite{liang2026toxpltc}, and further examined
task adaptation, bidirectional state-space modeling, multi-seed ensembling,
and OOF-based probability calibration. Unlike approaches that use a pretrained
protein language model merely as a fixed feature extractor, GHXTox preserves
residue-level ProtT5 representations, adapts them to peptide toxicity
prediction through LoRA, and subsequently models residue dependencies in both
the N-to-C and C-to-N directions using bidirectional Mamba.

The gain of the new 1D expert did not appear uniformly across
threshold-dependent metrics. At their respective operating points,
the previous ProtT5-TextCNN sequence model achieved BACC, MCC, AUROC, and
AUPRC values of 0.9317, 0.8586, 0.9724, and 0.9436, respectively, whereas the
LoRA-ProtT5--Bi-Mamba expert achieved 0.9300, 0.8562, 0.9761, and 0.9533.
Thus, BACC and MCC remained broadly comparable, while AUROC and AUPRC
increased by approximately 0.0037 and 0.0097, respectively.

The improvement was concentrated in probability ranking. This stronger ranking
stream benefits subsequent fusion with structural evidence and explains why
sequence-model comparisons should not rely on ACC or BACC alone.

\subsubsection{Task Adaptation of ProtT5 and the Final 1D Representation}
Existing peptide-toxicity predictors commonly employ protein language models
to generate fixed sequence representations. For example, StrucToxNet uses
ProtT5-derived sequence features within its graph representation, whereas
PeptiTox integrates ESM2 embeddings with geometry derived from predicted
structures \cite{jiao2025structoxnet,wang2025peptitox}. A major distinction
of GHXTox is that ProtT5 is adapted to the toxicity task through LoRA, while
its residue-level representations are preserved for downstream sequence
modeling.

This design serves two complementary purposes. First, low-rank updates allow
the pretrained semantic space to adapt to peptide toxicity prediction without
requiring full fine-tuning of the ProtT5 backbone. Second, peptide-level
pooling is deliberately postponed: contextualized residue states are passed
to the Bi-Mamba encoder, preserving position-dependent information and
allowing additional modeling of local and long-range residue dependencies.

Because LoRA and Bi-Mamba were not ablated independently, their contribution
is evaluated jointly.

\subsubsection{Bidirectional State-Space Modeling of Peptide Sequences}
After task-adaptive ProtT5 encoding, GHXTox employs bidirectional Mamba
rather than TextCNN or immediate peptide-level pooling for secondary sequence
modeling. This choice is motivated by the properties of peptides:
toxicity-related evidence may not be confined to a single local motif;
instead, combinations of charged, hydrophobic, aromatic, or other residues
may interact over distant sequence positions. Fixed-size convolutions can
capture such dependencies only through progressively enlarged receptive
fields, whereas a state-space model propagates contextual information through
recurrent state updates.

Although peptide sequences possess a natural N-to-C direction, toxicity
classification is not an autoregressive prediction task. GHXTox therefore
performs both forward and backward selective scans and integrates their
representations. Each residue can consequently be contextualized by
information from both termini rather than forcing the classifier to adopt a
single direction of information propagation.

Because no strict single-variable comparison between unidirectional and
bidirectional Mamba under otherwise identical conditions is available, we do
not assign a quantitative performance gain specifically to bidirectionality.
The complete LoRA-ProtT5--Bi-Mamba configuration is therefore evaluated as a
whole through group-aware OOF evaluation and controlled comparisons.

\subsubsection{Multi-Seed Ensembling, Calibration, and Controlled Negative Results}
After the basic sequence architecture had been established, we further
investigated model variance and probability reliability. The final 1D expert
averages the probabilities of three independently trained full-data models
with equal weights; no seed was selected on test performance. The 15-member
fold-checkpoint ensemble did not improve the OOF validation criteria and was
therefore not used.

Following seed averaging, training OOF predictions were used to fit the
monotonic Platt calibrator. This operation primarily adjusts the probability
scale without altering the ranking of samples. The observed results were
consistent with this role: the 1D Brier score decreased from 0.0445 to 0.0429
on Test1 and from 0.0491 to 0.0451 on Test2. After subsequent fusion with the
3D expert, the Brier scores were further reduced to 0.0410 and 0.0436,
respectively. Calibration therefore plays a substantive role in the pipeline:
it aligns the probability scales of the two experts before fusion.

Three intuitively attractive extensions were additionally evaluated but not
adopted. Conservative positive-class sequence perturbation shifted
predictions toward the positive class and degraded specificity and the Brier
score. Length-specific calibration or thresholding was unstable because of
increased sampling variance within smaller strata and did not consistently
outperform the global operating point. Finally, the 15-member fold ensemble
did not outperform the three full-data models.

These variants did not improve the final 1D configuration. The three-seed
full-data average with OOF calibration represents a simpler and more stable
solution and reduces the risk of selective reporting or continued optimization
against historically observed test sets.

\subsection{Analysis of the pLDDT-Aware Multi-Scale 3D Structural Expert}
Compared with the 1D expert, the principal methodological innovations of
GHXTox lie in its structural expert. Previous studies have already
demonstrated the value of predicted structures for toxicity prediction.
PeptiTox combines ESM2 semantics with geometric descriptors extracted from
predicted structures, whereas StrucToxNet further employs an equivariant
graph neural network to preserve consistency under rigid transformations of
the three-dimensional coordinates
\cite{wang2025peptitox,jiao2025structoxnet}. For short peptides, however, the
more fundamental question is which structural evidence should be trusted and
at which spatial scale it should be used.

The GHXTox structural expert is organized around this question. ESMFold
predictions are treated as uncertain structures whose pLDDT scores act as a
continuous reliability variable throughout geometric message passing,
residue-level gating, peptide-level gating, and training-time coordinate
perturbation. The model then extends beyond C$\alpha$-level
residue geometry by extracting local chemical pseudo-sites from full-atom PDB
structures and, when conformer ensembles are available, incorporating
statistics such as RMSF, distance variation, and contact occupancy through a
conservative residual pathway.

The 3D contribution therefore lies in a hierarchical structural
representation that organizes evidence by reliability and spatial scale, from
residue geometry and local side-chain chemistry to sampled conformational
variation.

\subsubsection{Effect of Structural-Confidence Modeling}
Structure-quality analysis first confirmed that pLDDT contains useful
information: peptides with higher mean pLDDT generally exhibit stronger
predictive performance. However, this does not imply that discarding
low-confidence samples is an optimal modeling strategy. In a controlled
high-pLDDT-only experiment, restricting training to high-confidence
structures substantially reduced performance over the complete test domain,
with MCC values of only 0.7232 on Test1 and 0.5593 on Test2.

Hard filtering removed difficult but informative samples along with uncertain
structures.

GHXTox therefore uses a continuous confidence-aware strategy. Low-confidence
edges remain in the graph but contribute weaker messages; residue-level gates
modulate structural evidence at individual positions; peptide-level gating
controls the overall use of structural context; and stronger coordinate
perturbations are applied to low-pLDDT residues during training.

This differs from approaches that analyze structure quality only after model
inference. In GHXTox, pLDDT is an active component of representation learning,
not a diagnostic variable appended after inference.

\subsubsection{Contribution of Full-Atom Chemical Sites}
A C$\alpha$-level residue graph effectively describes peptide compactness,
nonlocal contacts, and residue neighborhoods but compresses chemically
complex side chains into single residue nodes. To determine whether local
spatial chemistry provides additional discriminative information, we
introduced a full-atom-PDB-derived chemical-site residual on top of the
frozen C$\alpha$-EGNN. A strict paired five-fold design was adopted: in each
fold, the new model was initialized from the same control checkpoint,
while the sequence branch, C$\alpha$-EGNN, fusion module, and classifier were
frozen, leaving only the newly introduced chemical-site branch trainable.

Across the five CD-HIT homology-grouped folds, the complete chemical-site
model increased mean BACC from 0.9153 to 0.9171 ($\Delta=+0.0018$), F1 from
0.8790 to 0.8814 ($\Delta=+0.0023$), MCC from 0.8312 to 0.8342
($\Delta=+0.0030$), AUROC from 0.9687 to approximately 0.9692, and AUPRC from
0.9388 to 0.9401 ($\Delta=+0.0013$). More importantly, MCC, F1, and AUPRC
improved in all five folds.

Although modest in magnitude, the improvements were consistent across folds.
Because only the chemical-site branch was trainable, the result indicates that
local side-chain chemistry provides a small but reproducible signal beyond
residue-centered geometry.

Component-level ablations further support this interpretation. On the fixed
fold-0 subset, removing the second chemical site, orientation
invariants, either distance scale, explicit compatibility channels, pLDDT
weighting, or zero initialization decreased MCC. Keeping only the
raw-distance or only the normalized-distance representation reduced MCC by
approximately 0.0059 and 0.0058, respectively, whereas removing
interaction-type channels or pLDDT weighting each reduced MCC by
approximately 0.0057.

PeptiTox demonstrated the utility of geometric information from predicted
structures, whereas ToxiPep highlighted complementarity between local atomic
chemistry and sequence context
\cite{wang2025peptitox,guan2025toxipep}. GHXTox integrates these ideas within a
confidence-aware 3D structural expert. The resulting interaction channels
should nevertheless be interpreted as candidate spatial-chemical
relationships available to the model, rather than confirmed hydrogen bonds,
salt bridges, cation--$\pi$ interactions, or disulfide bonds.

\subsubsection{Sequence--Structure Interaction and Confidence Gating}
Local geometric evidence becomes meaningful only in the appropriate sequence
context. The same spatial distance or hydrophobic contact may have different
implications depending on residue identity and sequence position. GHXTox
therefore avoids simply concatenating sequence and structural vectors after
peptide-level pooling. Instead, contextualized sequence states act as queries
that selectively retrieve evidence from pLDDT-gated spatial residue
representations.

This idea is conceptually related to the context--chemistry alignment
emphasized by ToxiPep, but in GHXTox the interaction occurs within a predicted
three-dimensional structural expert and is further regulated by both
residue-level and peptide-level structural-confidence gates.

The residue-level gate modulates how strongly the structural representation
of each residue contributes to the subsequent interaction, whereas the
peptide-level gate controls the overall contribution of structural context.
Structural information is therefore not switched off when confidence
decreases; rather, its contribution is continuously attenuated at multiple
levels.

Together with the negative result of hard high-pLDDT-only training described
in Section 3.4.1, these observations suggest that the value of this design
is the learnable compromise it establishes between preserving structural
information and attenuating uncertain evidence.

Because no complete single-variable ablation of cross-attention and each
confidence gate under otherwise identical conditions is available, the overall
structural gain is not quantitatively decomposed among these components. The
evidence therefore supports the reliability-aware interaction
mechanism, comprising continuous pLDDT weighting, sequence--structure
cross-attention, and hierarchical gating.

\subsubsection{Effect of Conformer Statistics}
A single ESMFold prediction provides only one deterministic structural
anchor, whereas short peptides may exhibit substantial conformational
heterogeneity. GHXTox does not treat every generated conformer as an
additional labeled training example, nor does it repeatedly execute the
complete EGNN for every conformation. Instead, conformer ensembles are
compressed into statistics including RMSF, pairwise-distance variation,
contact occupancy at 6, 8, and 10~Å, conformer RMSD, and radius of
gyration, and these statistics are allowed to produce only a zero-initialized
logit residual on top of the single-structure predictor.

The multi-source branch covers 2{,}795 of the 6{,}387 training
peptides. At the unchanged 0.72/0.28 fusion and decision threshold, activating
the multi-source residual increased training-OOF MCC from 0.87255 to 0.87445
and BACC from 0.93220 to 0.93292, while reducing the Brier score from 0.04316
to 0.04309. The MCC difference was $+0.00190$ under 5{,}000 class-stratified
paired bootstrap resamples, with a 95\% interval of
$[-0.00043,\,0.00454]$ and a paired-randomization $p$ value of 0.203. AUROC
and AUPRC decreased by 0.00014 and 0.00015, respectively.

The gain remained limited: conformers showed substantial geometric variation,
whereas expert probabilities changed only marginally. This suggests
that the current bottleneck lies more in mapping conformational variation to
toxicity labels than in generating geometrically distinct conformers.
Conformer statistics produced small, inconsistent changes and were not used
during benchmark inference. For peptides without valid conformer statistics,
$\Delta z_{\mathrm{conf}} = 0$, and the prediction falls back exactly to the
single-structure pathway (the benchmark conformer ablation, alternative
aggregators, and training-only optimization variants are detailed in the
Supporting Information).

\subsubsection{Overall Performance of the Structural Expert}
After combining all components, the 3D-v2 structural expert achieved
BACC, MCC, AUROC, and AUPRC values of 0.9208, 0.8464, 0.9711, and 0.9474 on
Test1, compared with 0.9179, 0.8458, 0.9699, and 0.9426 for the historical
C$\alpha$-only 3D-v1 model. Test1 therefore showed a consistent but modest
improvement. Test2 exhibited a different trade-off: AUPRC increased from
0.6969 to 0.7241, whereas MCC decreased from 0.6320 to 0.6128.

This trade-off indicates that finer-grained structural modeling improves
positive-class probability ranking but does not guarantee simultaneous gains
in all metrics at a fixed operating point. It matters especially for Test2,
whose 46 toxic peptides mean that a small number of threshold-adjacent
samples can substantially alter MCC and F1.

The benefit of the 3D-v2 expert was dataset-dependent. Test1 improved across
the reported metrics, whereas on Test2 AUPRC increased but the fixed-threshold
MCC decreased; these gains were measured under group-aware training OOF
evaluation.

\subsection{Robustness, Statistical Uncertainty, and Applicability Analysis}
Point estimates alone are insufficient for distinguishing a stable model
improvement from variation caused by finite samples. We therefore use
multi-seed averaging for the 1D expert and use paired, sample-level bootstrap
resampling to compare the multi-source residual with its same-checkpoint
zero-conformer OOF baseline. All comparisons preserve the pairing between the
two predictions for each training peptide.

\subsubsection{Multi-Seed Reproducibility}
Three full-training 1D models initialized with seeds 42, 123, and 2025 are
averaged before calibration. This ensemble is the final predictor; no seed
is selected according to Test1 or Test2 performance. A 15-member ensemble of
fold-specific checkpoints was also examined but not adopted because its
members had each seen only approximately 80\% of the training set and the
aggregate did not improve the frozen validation criteria.

\subsubsection{Paired Uncertainty and Point-Estimate Comparison}
Paired-bootstrap and point-estimate comparisons involving the multi-source
conformer variant and the preceding pilot model are reported in the
Supporting Information; the variant was not part of the final benchmark
predictor.

\subsubsection{Selective Prediction and Risk--Coverage Analysis}
The training-OOF analysis showed that proximity to the decision
boundary provides an informative ranking of prediction risk. As coverage was
reduced from 100\% to 95\%, 90\%, 85\%, and 80\%, the accepted-sample error
decreased from 5.12\% to 3.66\%, 2.92\%, 2.41\%, and 2.05\%, respectively;
MCC increased from 0.8733 to 0.9082, 0.9257, 0.9387, and 0.9481. The discrete
OOF area under the risk--coverage curve was 0.01537. At the prespecified 90\%
operating point, the realized OOF coverage was 89.995\% (5{,}748 of 6{,}387

$d_{\mathrm{OOF}}^{*}=0.47361765874159856$. Equal-count random rejection at
this point retained an accepted error of 5.12\%; the random-minus-selective
difference was 2.19 percentage points (95\% paired-bootstrap CI,
1.81--2.57). Random rejection produced no comparable improvement, indicating
that the uncertainty ranking preferentially removed difficult samples.

to 0.9629. Sensitivity and specificity increased to 0.9397 and 0.9860,
respectively. The full-minus-accepted error reduction was 2.53 percentage
points (95\% paired-bootstrap CI, 1.62--3.51), whereas equal-count random
rejection retained an average error of 5.24\% and an MCC of 0.8705.

\subsection{Interpretability and Representative Case Studies}
The interpretation experiments used the model and the
prespecified case-selection rule described above. The analysis included 460
residue occlusions across 16 peptides, unperturbed confidence gates for all
1{,}708 benchmark records, 445 individual chemical-site deletions, 121
category-level deletions, and 424 residue-level site deletions. Before
perturbation, baseline probabilities were recomputed and compared with the
stored prediction vectors. The maximum absolute discrepancy for the 1D
expert within the case cohort was $3.45\times10^{-4}$; the corresponding 3D
discrepancies were $5.49\times10^{-4}$ on Test1 and $1.14\times10^{-5}$ on
Test2. None of these differences changed a standalone 3D or fused decision.
The perturbation baselines therefore preserved the stored decision states,
with the larger Test1 discrepancy reported as bounded cross-environment
numerical drift.

\subsubsection{Residue-Level Interpretation of the 1D Expert}
The residue-occlusion profiles were heterogeneous across peptides and seeds.
Across the 16 prespecified cases, the mean pairwise Spearman correlation of
absolute per-residue logit effects was 0.118 (case-level range, $-0.077$ to
0.422), whereas the mean residue-wise sign-agreement fraction was 0.740
(range, 0.667--0.796). Direction was therefore more stable across seeds than
the fine-grained ranking of residue magnitudes. The reserved maps emphasize
local effects with consistent signs rather than implying a single stable
ranking across all residues.

Several cases nevertheless contained large, directionally consistent effects.
For the Test2 toxic peptide \texttt{sequence\_34\_34}, occlusion of Asn9
reduced the calibrated 1D probability from 0.5447 to 0.0412
($\Delta p_{1D}=+0.5035$), with all three seeds agreeing on the sign. In the
Test1 toxic case \texttt{peptide\_199}, occlusion of Ser24 reduced the score
from 0.3687 to 0.1114 ($\Delta p_{1D}=+0.2573$), again with complete sign
agreement. Effects were not uniformly toxicity-supporting: in the Test2 toxic
case \texttt{sequence\_23\_23}, occlusion of Ile5 increased the 1D probability
from 0.2807 to 0.4819 ($\Delta p_{1D}=-0.2013$).

These cases illustrate large, context-dependent sensitivities in the 1D
output; they do not identify experimentally validated toxic residues.

\subsubsection{Structural Confidence and Chemical-Site Sensitivity}
Mean pLDDT was strongly associated with the learned global structural gate.
Spearman correlations were
0.960 on Test1 (95\% peptide-bootstrap interval, 0.953--0.966) and 0.950 on
Test2 (0.937--0.959); the pooled descriptive value was 0.957
(0.951--0.961). In contrast, the mean within-peptide correlation between
residue pLDDT and node gates was small: 0.049 on Test1 (0.030--0.068) and
0.064 on Test2 (0.036--0.092). Thus, global gating closely tracked confidence
at the peptide level, whereas the weak local correlations are consistent with
residue gates also reflecting geometric and contextual features.

Most individual-site deletions produced small changes, but the distribution
had a pronounced upper tail. Across 445 deletions, the median and mean absolute changes in
$p_{3D}$ were 0.00048 and 0.00997, respectively; the 95th percentile was
0.0570 and the maximum was 0.2994. Nine individual deletions moved the
recomputed fused probability across the decision threshold when the 1D
probability was held fixed. At category level, hydrophobic-site deletion
had the largest mean absolute change among the eight modeled categories
(0.126 across 16 case-level deletions), followed by aromatic sites (0.0687),
whereas sulfur-site deletion had the smallest mean absolute change (0.00179).
Because the analysis used the selected 16-peptide cohort, these category-level
estimates are descriptive, not population effects.

The largest individual-site effect occurred at the aromatic/hydrophobic Phe8
site in the matched cache-covered cases \texttt{peptide\_846} and
\texttt{sequence\_388\_388}: deletion increased $p_{3D}$ from approximately
0.2405 to 0.5399, giving $\Delta p_{3D}\approx-0.2994$. Conversely, deletion
of the hydrophobic/conformational Pro19 site in \texttt{peptide\_199}
decreased $p_{3D}$ from 0.3443 to 0.1163
($\Delta p_{3D}=+0.2280$).

The opposing directions show that the deletion score depends on the
surrounding peptide context and cannot be read as an intrinsic toxicity label
for a functional group.

\subsubsection{Representative Expert-Disagreement Cases}
The aggregate disagreement pattern is reported in Section 3.2.3. Here, we
focus on representative cases in which the two experts provide
opposing evidence and the fixed fusion either rescues or reverses a correct
standalone decision.

The toxic Test2 peptide \texttt{sequence\_23\_23} illustrates a structural
rescue. Its 1D probability was 0.2806, below the standalone sequence threshold,
whereas the 3D probability was 0.9954. The fixed 0.72/0.28 fusion yielded
$p_{\mathrm{fused}}=0.4808$, moving the final prediction just above the
predeclared threshold and restoring the correct toxic classification. The
residue-occlusion analysis also showed that zeroing the contextual vector at
Ile5 increased the 1D score, underscoring that the sequence evidence in this
case was both weak and context dependent.

The toxic peptide \texttt{sequence\_34\_34} shows the opposite outcome. Its
1D probability of 0.5447 was correct at the standalone threshold, but its 3D
probability was $3.0\times10^{-5}$. Fusion reduced the probability to 0.3922
and produced a false negative. In the 1D sensitivity analysis, occluding Asn9
reduced the calibrated sequence probability by 0.5035 with complete
cross-seed sign agreement, indicating that strong sequence evidence can be
overridden when the structural expert supplies sufficiently strong opposing
evidence.

These cases do not establish a biological mechanism. They demonstrate the
operational consequence of fixed late fusion: complementary evidence can
correct an expert-specific error, but the same rule can also regress an
otherwise correct decision. Figure~\ref{fig:expert_disagreement_cases}
reserves the case-level presentation without repeating the aggregate counts
shown in Figure~\ref{fig:expert_complementarity}.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Directions}
Several limitations remain. Test1 and Test2 were observed during earlier model
development, so they are frozen historical benchmarks rather than blinded
external test sets, and Test2 contains only 46 toxic peptides, so its SN,
BACC, and MCC are sensitive to a few prediction changes. Stronger
generalization evidence will require a new dataset evaluated under a
prefrozen protocol.

Structural predictions remain uncertain: pLDDT reflects predicted confidence
rather than experimental accuracy or conformational flexibility, and
full-atom-derived chemical sites are candidate spatial-chemical relationships
rather than confirmed bonds. Conformer coverage is 43.16\% of Test1 and
48.80\% of Test2, with generator identity partly coupled to peptide length;
the conformer pathway is therefore a conditional refinement, not a universal
source of improvement. More flexible residual variants did not yield a stable
multimetric improvement, and the single sample-level toxicity label does not
specify which conformer or microstate should carry the activity.

The two experts are trained separately and combined with a fixed fusion weight
that cannot adapt per sample. Interpretability analyses are limited in scope:
residue occlusion covers a 16-peptide cohort with modest cross-seed rank
agreement, and chemical-site deletion operates on predicted-structure
features. Future work should therefore prioritize a new independent external
dataset, better-controlled same-sample conformer sets, and representation
learning for the conformer pathway rather than further fixed aggregators;
orthogonal attribution methods and, where feasible, mutational or toxicity
experiments could then test the resulting hypotheses.

\section{CONCLUSIONS}
GHXTox combines a task-adaptive LoRA-ProtT5--Bi-Mamba sequence expert with a
pLDDT-aware multi-scale structural expert through a fixed probability fusion
protocol. The structural expert integrates residue geometry, full-atom-derived
chemical sites, and sequence-conditioned structural evidence, while
multi-conformer information is restricted to training
and controlled ablation. The final predictor uses the structural expert's
single-structure base probability. The 1D/3D
weights (0.72/0.28) and the decision threshold (0.47022256531284556) were
fixed from training OOF predictions before benchmark evaluation.

On the frozen historical Test1 and Test2 benchmarks, the model reaches MCC
values of 0.8705 and 0.6673 and AUPRC values of 0.9674 and 0.8046, and the
final fusion improves AUROC, AUPRC, and the Brier score relative to the
calibrated 1D stream. The 3D expert correctly classified 36.92\% and 37.50\%
of the respective Test1 and Test2 errors made by the 1D expert, with most of
these structural rescues occurring among non-toxic peptides. An OOF-frozen
selective policy further reduced accepted-sample error to 2.71\% and 3.16\%
at realized coverages of 88.54\% and 86.94\%, respectively, without
benchmark-specific cutoff selection. Conformer-derived information produced
only modest and non-uniform improvements, indicating that mapping
conformational variation to toxicity-relevant evidence remains an important
limitation. The interpretability analyses describe model sensitivity rather
than causal mechanisms.

Several limitations remain: both benchmarks were observed during earlier model
development, the selective rule has not been prospectively validated, and the
structures are predicted rather than experimental.
Future work should prioritize a new
independent external dataset, better-controlled same-sample conformer sets,
and representation learning for the conformer pathway, followed where feasible
by experimental validation.
