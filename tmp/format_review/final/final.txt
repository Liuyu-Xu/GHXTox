Abstract.Peptide toxicity is a major safety concern in peptide-based drug development,
motivating accurate computational screening methods. Existing methods have begun
to explore multi-dimensional (multimodal) feature fusion and toxicity prediction based
on the three-dimensional (3D) conformations of peptides, yet most of them still un-
derutilize 3D structural information, and systematic multi-dimensional fusion remains
rare. Here, we propose GHXTox, a hierarchical dual-level fusion framework in which
a 3D structural expert first integrates sequence semantics with spatial structural fea-
tures through predicted local-distance difference test (pLDDT)-aware cross-attention,
and is then fused at the probability level with a task-adaptive one-dimensional (1D)
sequence expert using weights frozen on training out-of-fold (OOF) predictions. The
1D branch analyzes sequences with a protein language model (ProtT5 fine-tuned with
low-rank adaptation, LoRA) and a bidirectional Mamba state-space model. The 3D ex-
pert integrates an ESMFold-derived structural anchor, pLDDT-aware residue geometry,
and full-atom-derived chemical sites. A separate residual head was trained on multi-
conformer descriptors while the single-structure backbone was frozen. This residual is
disabled in the canonical predictor and is reported only in controlled ablations; bench-
mark and public inference use the single-structure base logits. On the frozen historical
benchmark test sets, GHXTox achieves MCC values of 0.8705 and 0.6673 and AUPRC
values of 0.9674 and 0.8046 on Test1 and Test2, respectively. Further analyses char-
acterize residue-level sequence sensitivity, confidence–gate associations, chemical-site
perturbations, expert disagreement, and an OOF-frozen selective-prediction rule. At re-
alized coverages of 88.54% and 86.94%, the selective rule reduced the accepted-sample
error to 2.71% and 3.16% on Test1 and Test2 without benchmark-specific cutoff selec-
tion. These findings are interpreted as model-level evidence rather than causal biological
mechanisms. Frozen-model analyses indicate that the conformer contribution is small
and non-uniform: under the structural expert, substantial geometric variation does not
necessarily translate into stable outcome-aligned probability variation.
Keywords: peptide toxicity prediction; multimodal deep learning; protein language
model; bidirectional Mamba; pLDDT-aware multi-scale 3D modeling; interpretability

1 INTRODUCTION
    Peptides are increasingly used as therapeutic candidates because of their high tar-
get specificity and functional diversity [Muttenthaler et al.(2021)Muttenthaler, King,



                                            1
Adams, and Alewood]. However, peptide toxicity remains a major safety concern dur-
ing drug development, and the accurate and efficient identification of toxic peptides is a
major bottleneck in peptide-based drug discovery [Zhang et al.(2025)Zhang, Ren, and
Liang]. Because conventional experimental toxicity assays are costly, time-consuming,
and difficult to scale, reliable computational predictors are highly desirable [Zhang
et al.(2025)Zhang, Ren, and Liang].
    Computational peptide toxicity prediction has evolved from traditional machine
learning methods based on handcrafted physicochemical features toward deep learn-
ing approaches with stronger representation-learning capability. Current mainstream
methods can be roughly grouped into two categories. The first category operates on
sequence-level (one-dimensional, 1D) and/or atomic-level (two-dimensional, 2D) rep-
resentations. For example, ToxMSRC combines continuous bag-of-words (CBOW)
embeddings, multi-scale convolutional neural networks, and a bidirectional long short-
term memory network with residual connections, achieving strong performance on the
benchmark dataset [Zhang et al.(2025)Zhang, Ren, and Liang]. ToxPLTC integrates
pooled ProtT5 embeddings, borderline-SMOTE augmentation, and a text convolutional
neural network [Liang and Wang(2026)Liang, and Wang]. ToxiPep fuses context-
aware sequence representations with atomic-level graphs through a cross-attention
mechanism [Guan et al.(2025)Guan, Xie, Meng, Yao, Yu, Chiang, Lee, and Wang].
HyPepTox-Fuse further combines embeddings from multiple protein language models
with conventional descriptors [Tran et al.(2025)Tran, Pham, Nguyen, Wei, and Man-
avalan]. Although these models achieve strong performance within their respective
modalities, they largely neglect the three-dimensional (3D) conformations of peptides,
which are closely related to toxicity; cross-dimensional information interaction and
mutual validation remain limited, and their interpretability is mostly confined to the
sequence level.
    The second category attempts to exploit 3D structural information. PeptiTox uses
ESM2 sequence embeddings together with ESMFold-predicted 3D structures and ge-
ometric graph neural networks [Wang et al.(2025)Wang, Li, Wang, Cao, Xiong, and
Wei]. StrucToxNet feeds ProtT5 sequence embeddings and ESMFold-predicted struc-
tures into an equivariant graph neural network (EGNN) and systematically analyzes
structural quality [Jiao et al.(2025)Jiao, Ye, Sakurai, Zou, Han, and Zhan]. These
studies demonstrate the value of 3D conformations for toxicity prediction. However,
these predictors still depend on predicted structures whose reliability can vary for short
peptides, and both evaluate one predicted conformation per sequence rather than an
explicit conformer ensemble. Existing designs use predicted-structure confidence pri-
marily for filtering or structural-quality analysis rather than as a continuous message-
level reliability signal [Wang et al.(2025)Wang, Li, Wang, Cao, Xiong, and Wei, Jiao
et al.(2025)Jiao, Ye, Sakurai, Zou, Han, and Zhan, Gulsevin and Meiler(2022)Gulsevin,
and Meiler]. Moreover, the fusion between the sequence and structure branches re-
mains relatively shallow, and no systematic multi-dimensional fusion that combines a
task-adaptive sequence model with confidence-aware multi-scale structure modeling has
been established.
    To address these limitations, we propose GHXTox, an interpretable hierarchical dual-




                                            2
level fusion framework for peptide toxicity prediction. A task-adaptive 1D sequence
expert combines a ProtT5 protein language model, adapted by low-rank adaptation
(LoRA), with a bidirectional Mamba state-space encoder and three-seed ensembling [El-
naggar et al.(2022)Elnaggar, Heinzinger, Dallago, et al. others, Hu et al.(2022)Hu,
Shen, Wallis, Allen-Zhu, Li, Wang, Wang, and Chen, Gu and Dao(2024)Gu, and Dao].
A sequence-enhanced 3D structural expert integrates sequence semantics with spatial
structure through pLDDT-aware cross-attention, ESMFold-derived geometry, full-atom
chemical sites, and confidence gating across multiple structural scales; PepFlow and
AlphaFold2/ColabFold conformer ensembles were used to train and evaluate a sepa-
rate residual head while the single-structure backbone remained frozen; this head is
retained only for controlled ablations, and the final predictor uses the single-structure
ESMFold anchor [Lin et al.(2023)Lin, Akin, Rao, Hie, Zhu, Lu, et al. others, Jumper
et al.(2021)Jumper, Evans, Pritzel, et al. others, Mirdita et al.(2022)Mirdita, Schütze,
Moriwaki, et al. others, Abdin and Kim(2024)Abdin, and Kim]. The two expert prob-
abilities are fused at the probability level with weights fixed on training out-of-fold
(OOF) predictions; the 1D probabilities are first calibrated on the same OOF table,
and no benchmark labels are used during this selection. Gated sequence–structure
cross-attention inside the structural expert further informs the interaction design [Zhang
and Zhang(2026)Zhang, and Zhang]. Ablation experiments show that the contribu-
tion of structural evidence is dataset- and metric-dependent: fusion improves several
fixed-threshold and probability-quality measures on Test1 while maintaining broadly
comparable performance on Test2. Post hoc analyses characterize residue-level sensi-
tivity, confidence gating, chemical-site perturbations, and expert disagreement without
assigning causal biological meaning to these signals. The framework is evaluated on
the frozen historical benchmarks described in Section 2.1.
    In summary, the main contributions of this work are threefold: (1) we propose GHX-
Tox, an interpretable hierarchical dual-level fusion framework that explores the comple-
mentary joint use of one-dimensional sequence and three-dimensional conformational
information, integrating a task-adaptive 1D protein language model with pLDDT-aware
multi-scale 3D structure modeling and combining the two experts at the probability level
(Sections 2.5 and 3.2); (2) the 3D branch designs a multi-scale structural information
extraction strategy spanning from local residue functional groups to the whole peptide,
and incorporates pLDDT confidence into structural feature processing through contin-
uous down-weighting and dynamic gating; and (3) extensive experiments on the frozen
historical benchmark test sets show that GHXTox improves several fixed-threshold and
probability-quality measures on Test1 while maintaining broadly comparable perfor-
mance on Test2, while ablation, robustness, and interpretability analyses characterize
the conditions under which sequence and structural evidence are complementary.

2 MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1 Datasets
    We evaluate GHXTox on the benchmark datasets constructed by CAPTP [Jiao
et al.(2024)Jiao, Ye, Sakurai, Zou, and Liu] and subsequently adopted by ToxM-
SRC [Zhang et al.(2025)Zhang, Ren, and Liang]. Following the data construction pro-




                                            3
tocol of ToxMSRC [Zhang et al.(2025)Zhang, Ren, and Liang], toxic peptide sequences
were merged from three databases, namely CSM-Toxin [Morozov et al.(2023)Morozov,
Rodrigues, and Ascher], ToxinPred2 [Sharma et al.(2022)Sharma, Naorem, Jain, and
Raghava], and ATSE [Wei et al.(2021)Wei, Ye, Xue, Sakurai, and Wei], and se-
quences longer than 50 residues were removed. Non-toxic sequences were retrieved
from UniProt [The UniProt Consortium(2019)] using the query “NOT KW-0800 AND
NOT KW-0020 AND reviewed:true”, and sequences containing non-standard amino
acids or exceeding 50 residues were excluded. After combining the two groups and
removing samples with conflicting labels, CD-HIT [Fu et al.(2012)Fu, Niu, Zhu, Wu,
and Li] was applied with a sequence-identity threshold of 0.9 to eliminate redundant
sequences, yielding 2,138 toxic and 5,375 non-toxic peptides. Following the same par-
tition as ToxMSRC [Zhang et al.(2025)Zhang, Ren, and Liang], we used 85% of the
data as the training set and the remaining 15% as the benchmark Test1 set, resulting
in 1,818 toxic and 4,569 non-toxic training peptides and 320 toxic and 806 non-toxic
Test1 peptides. An additional benchmark Test2 set, containing 46 toxic and 536 non-
toxic peptides, is used for frozen historical evaluation. Both Test1 and Test2 had been
observed during historical project development and are therefore treated as frozen his-
torical benchmarks rather than newly blinded external validation sets; their labels were
not used in the formal OOF calibration, fusion-weight selection, or threshold-freezing
procedure. ToxinPred 3.0 [Rathore et al.(2024)Rathore, Choudhury, Arora, Tijare, and
Raghava] was consulted as an external method and data-source reference during project
development, but its records were not added to the present training set or to the formal
Test1/Test2 result tables. Table 1 summarizes the statistics of the datasets used in this
study.

2.2 Overview of the Model
    GHXTox is a hierarchical dual-level fusion framework for peptide toxicity prediction:
a task-adaptive one-dimensional (1D) sequence expert and a sequence-enhanced three-
dimensional (3D) structural expert generate independent probabilities, interact internally
at the feature level, and are combined at the probability level.
    The 1D expert processes the peptide sequence with a LoRA-adapted ProtT5 encoder
[Elnaggar et al.(2022)Elnaggar, Heinzinger, Dallago, et al. others, Hu et al.(2022)Hu,
Shen, Wallis, Allen-Zhu, Li, Wang, Wang, and Chen] followed by a bidirectional Mamba
state-space model [Gu and Dao(2024)Gu, and Dao], averages three seed models, and
applies OOF-fitted Platt calibration to produce 𝑝 1D (Section 2.3). The 3D expert
integrates sequence semantics, residue geometry, full-atom chemical sites, and pLDDT
confidence across residue, local-spatial, chemical-site, and peptide scales, combines
sequence and spatial states through pLDDT-aware cross-attention, and emits 𝑝 SE3D
(Section 2.4).
    The two probabilities are combined at the probability level; the fusion rule, the
decision threshold, and the final output are described in Section 2.5. The complete
inference path is summarized in Figure 1.

2.3 Task-Adaptive 1D Sequence Modeling
    The 1D sequence expert learns toxicity-related contextual semantics directly from
peptide sequences. Instead of reducing a frozen protein-language-model output to
a fixed mean vector, GHXTox preserves the residue-level representations of ProtT5,
adapts the pretrained encoder to the toxicity task through low-rank adaptation (LoRA),
models residue dependencies with a bidirectional Mamba encoder, and aggregates the
residue states by masked mean pooling before classification. Three full-data models with
different random seeds are combined by equal-weight probability averaging followed by
Platt calibration fitted exclusively on training out-of-fold (OOF) predictions.
    Each residue is first written in the space-separated format expected by the ProtT5
tokenizer. A binary residue-validity mask is constructed from the raw residue count,
applied to invalid positions before each selective state-space scan, and reused in the
final pooling; canonical inference follows the fixed batch-construction procedure used
to generate the reported predictions. The tokenized sequence is processed by the
ProtT5-XL-UniRef50 encoder [Elnaggar et al.(2022)Elnaggar, Heinzinger, Dallago,
et al. others] with LoRA, yielding residue-level representations that retain position-
specific contextual semantics. To adapt this general-purpose encoder to the toxicity
task, LoRA [Hu et al.(2022)Hu, Shen, Wallis, Allen-Zhu, Li, Wang, Wang, and Chen]
is applied to all of its linear layers with rank 𝑟 = 8, scaling 𝛼 = 16, and dropout 0.1: the
pretrained weights stay frozen, and only the zero-initialized low-rank update (W = W0 +
𝛼
 𝑟 BA) and the downstream modules are trainable. The 1024-dimensional representations
are then projected into the 192-dimensional Mamba hidden space through a layer-norm–
GELU–dropout block.
    Two bidirectional Mamba layers [Gu and Dao(2024)Gu, and Dao] are then stacked
after the projection block to capture the sequential dependencies relevant to peptide
toxicity. Each directional block follows the standard selective state-space formulation
with input-dependent discretization and a 16-dimensional recurrent state; forward and
backward scans are applied to the masked sequence, and their outputs are concatenated,
fused by a linear layer, and added through a residual connection, so every residue
is contextualized by both its N-terminal and C-terminal contexts. Invalid positions
are masked before the selective scan and excluded from masked pooling; because the
depthwise convolution precedes this scan mask in the implementation, the reported
inference protocol retains the canonical batch construction. The resulting residue states
are aggregated by masked mean pooling over the valid positions, and a layer-normalized
single-output linear head yields the uncalibrated toxicity probability. Because non-toxic
peptides far outnumber toxic peptides in the training data, a positive-class-weighted
binary cross-entropy loss is used instead of SMOTE or Borderline-SMOTE, with the
positive-class weight 𝑤 + = 𝑁− /𝑁+ recomputed from each fold’s training partition; this
emphasizes the minority class without generating synthetic samples or leaking held-out
information.
    To reduce the variance of a single training run, three 1D models are trained under
identical settings with random seeds 42, 123, and 2025, and the ensemble score is the
equal-weight arithmetic average of the three full-data models; no seed is selected on test
performance and no seed-specific weight is introduced. Although averaging reduces

variance, neural-network probabilities may remain miscalibrated, so a monotonic two-
parameter Platt calibrator [Platt(1999)] is fitted to the training OOF scores (generated
with the fixed group-aware five-fold partition, Section 2.5) and applied to the three-seed
average at inference. Because the calibration is monotonic, it preserves the sample
ranking. Test1/Test2 labels are not used for member selection, calibration fitting, or
threshold optimization. The calibrated probability 𝑝 1D is the sequence-expert output
used in the top-level fusion (Section 2.5); no additional calibrator is fitted after fusion.

2.4 pLDDT-Aware Multi-Scale 3D Structural Modeling
    Peptide toxicity is influenced not only by amino acid composition and linear residue
order but also by the three-dimensional (3D) arrangement of charged groups, hydropho-
bic regions, aromatic systems, and sulfur-containing moieties. Because peptides are
generally short, conformationally flexible, and poorly covered by experimentally deter-
mined structures, treating a single predicted conformation as an error-free structural
observation may amplify structure-prediction errors, whereas C𝛼-only or peptide-level
summary representations fail to capture the local spatial-chemical environments that
are relevant to toxicity [Wang et al.(2025)Wang, Li, Wang, Cao, Xiong, and Wei, Jiao
et al.(2025)Jiao, Ye, Sakurai, Zou, Han, and Zhan, Gulsevin and Meiler(2022)Gulsevin,
and Meiler].
    To address these limitations, GHXTox employs a pLDDT-aware multi-scale struc-
tural expert that captures complementary single-structure information at four levels:
residue semantics, spatial residue neighborhoods, full-atom chemical sites, and peptide-
level global properties. Operationally, ESMFold provides a full-atom structural anchor
and residue-level pLDDT values, while frozen ESM2 embeddings and explicit residue
descriptors provide the internal sequence-semantic states. C𝛼 geometry is encoded on
a hybrid sequence/spatial-neighbor graph by a pLDDT-weighted equivariant network,
and full-atom chemical pseudo-sites provide a zero-initialized residue-level correction.
Contextual sequence states then query the confidence-gated spatial states through cross-
attention; masked residue pooling and peptide-level physicochemical descriptors pro-
duce the structural logit. A separately trained, availability-masked conformer-ensemble
residual can modify this logit only in controlled ablations and is disabled in the canonical



                                             6
benchmark and public-inference pathway.
    Sections 2.4.1–2.4.6 specify this pipeline in order: single-structure prediction and
conformer preparation, residue feature construction and the hybrid geometric graph,
pLDDT-aware equivariant encoding, full-atom chemical-site message passing and the
optional conformer residual, sequence–structure cross-attention, and global feature in-
tegration with classification. Throughout, pLDDT is treated as a continuous reliability
signal rather than a hard filter. It enters the residue and edge features, weights geometric
and chemical-site messages, and controls residue- and peptide-level structural gates.
Low-confidence structural evidence is thereby down-weighted rather than removed,
preserving structural coverage while limiting the influence of uncertain coordinates.
pLDDT is interpreted here as a local confidence estimate produced by the structure-
prediction model, not as an experimentally determined measure of structural accuracy,
conformational flexibility, or biological activity.
2.4.1 Peptide Structure Prediction and Conformer Preparation
     For a peptide sequence of length 𝐿, 𝑆 = (𝑎 1 , 𝑎 2 , . . . , 𝑎 𝐿 ), ESMFold is first used
to predict its three-dimensional structure [Lin et al.(2023)Lin, Akin, Rao, Hie, Zhu,
Lu, et al. others]. The predicted structure is stored as a full-atom PDB file, from
which residue-level C𝛼 coordinates, backbone coordinates, full-atom-derived chemical-
site coordinates, and pLDDT scores are extracted. The C𝛼 coordinates are collected
as X = [x1 , . . . , x 𝐿 ] ⊤ ∈ R 𝐿×3 , A denotes the full-atom-PDB-derived backbone and
chemical-site geometry, and q = (𝑞 1 , . . . , 𝑞 𝐿 ) ∈ [0, 1] 𝐿 contains the residue-level
pLDDT confidence scores rescaled to [0, 1], where 𝑞𝑖 denotes the residue-level pLDDT
score of residue 𝑖. The C𝛼 coordinates serve as the primary spatial variables of the
residue-level equivariant graph, whereas the full-atom coordinates are used to construct
local chemical sites. Before geometric encoding, the coordinates are centered at the
peptide centroid to remove the effect of absolute translation.
     A single predicted structure provides a common structural anchor but cannot repre-
sent potential conformational heterogeneity. The conformer-residual stage was therefore
trained, with the single-structure spatial backbone, chemical-site branch, and base clas-
sifier frozen, on a multi-source conformer variant using a 2,795-peptide cohort with up
to eight validated, aligned conformers per peptide. PepFlow is a generalized Boltzmann
generator that performs direct all-atom conformational-ensemble sampling from the
peptide energy landscape rather than predicting a single structure; it has been shown
to efficiently recapitulate experimental ensembles of short linear motifs (SLiMs, typ-
ically 3–15 residues) and to generate all-atom conformations for peptides of different
lengths [Abdin and Kim(2024)Abdin, and Kim]. Short-peptide conformations are there-
fore provided by PepFlow. In our pipeline, up to eight conformations are retained per
input peptide, and PepFlow is applied to peptides of at most 15 residues (the actual
training cohort spans 11–15 residues and comprises 761 records with eight conformers
each). Longer peptides are provided by AlphaFold2/ColabFold: AlphaFold2 is a high-
accuracy single-structure predictor [Jumper et al.(2021)Jumper, Evans, Pritzel, et al.
others], and its peptide-structure performance has been systematically evaluated for
peptides of 16–60 residues [Gulsevin and Meiler(2022)Gulsevin, and Meiler], an inter-
val that covers the 16–30-residue cohort; ColabFold, an MSA-accelerated AlphaFold2



                                              7
implementation [Mirdita et al.(2022)Mirdita, Schütze, Moriwaki, et al. others], is run in
our pipeline with five models and multiple seeds to generate candidate conformations.
These candidates are filtered by an average-pLDDT quality floor and then reduced to
eight diverse conformers per peptide by farthest-point sampling on Kabsch-aligned C𝛼-
RMSD [Kabsch(1976)], yielding 2,034 training records. These sources are represented
explicitly in the residual head; nevertheless, generator identity remains partially coupled
to peptide length and is treated as a limitation rather than as an independently identified
causal factor.

evidence of structural rigidity; the model draws no flexibility inference from the absence
of conformers and simply falls back to the single-structure prediction.
     The generated conformers are computational samples rather than experimentally
determined trajectories or rigorous thermodynamic ensembles. Consequently, RMSF,
contact occupancy, and distance variance are interpreted as sampling-derived descriptors
of conformational variation rather than direct measurements of peptide dynamics in
solution.

residue-level sequence–structure alignment inside the structural expert.

overfitting risk on short and uncertain predicted peptide structures. Unlike approaches
that simply append pLDDT as an ordinary node feature, GHXTox uses pLDDT to di-

influence representation learning at the propagation stage. Low pLDDT does not imply
that a residue is biologically unimportant; it may reflect prediction error or genuine
conformational flexibility, so the model reduces the reliability of uncertain coordinate
evidence while preserving the residue’s sequence-semantic and physicochemical fea-
tures.
2.4.4 Full-Atom Chemical-Site Message Passing
    A C𝛼-based residue graph captures the global fold and spatial neighborhoods but
cannot resolve chemically distinct moieties within the same side chain. For example,
tyrosine contains both an aromatic ring and a hydroxyl group, whereas tryptophan con-
tains an aromatic system and an indole nitrogen. Compressing each side chain into a
single centroid would mix these chemically different moieties. GHXTox therefore ex-
tracts chemistry-aware pseudo-sites from the predicted full-atom PDB structure and pro-
cesses them with a dedicated local message-passing branch [Gilmer et al.(2017)Gilmer,
Schoenholz, Riley, Vinyals, and Dahl].

identity is represented by an eight-dimensional multi-hot vector t𝑖𝑟 ∈ {0, 1}8 allowing

and conformational, where 𝑟 indexes the chemical site within residue 𝑖. Multiple types
may be assigned to the same site: for example, the cysteine sulfur site is labeled as donor,
hydrophobic, and sulfur-containing, whereas the proline ring is labeled as hydrophobic




                                            11
and conformationally constrained; the aromatic ring and hydroxyl group of tyrosine are
represented as separate sites, as are the aromatic system and the indole NE1 nitrogen
(donor) of tryptophan.

2.5 Fusion and Final Output
    The 1D expert emits the OOF-calibrated probability 𝑝 1D (Section 2.3), and the
structural expert emits 𝑝 SE3D (Section 2.4). The two probabilities are fused linearly in
probability space with weights frozen on training out-of-fold (OOF) predictions:

                            𝑝 GHXTox = 0.72 𝑝 1D + 0.28 𝑝 SE3D ,                           (17)

The fused probability 𝑝 GHXTox is the model’s continuous toxicity-probability output; it
is binarized directly at the OOF-frozen threshold, predicting toxic ( 𝑦ˆ = 1) if 𝑝 GHXTox ≥
0.47022256531284556 and non-toxic ( 𝑦ˆ = 0) otherwise. The default output is therefore
( 𝑝 GHXTox , 𝑦ˆ ). Experiments showed that directly emitting the fused probability yields
the best overall performance, so no additional network or calibration is applied after
fusion.
     The two experts were trained independently because they differ in input represen-
tation, parameter scale, and optimization behavior (the 1D and 3D loss, calibration,
and training details are described in Sections 2.3 and 2.4). Group-aware training OOF
predictions were used for calibration, fusion-weight selection, and threshold determina-
tion, and Test1/Test2 labels were excluded from all of these procedures. The training



                                             15
sequences were clustered by CD-HIT at a sequence-identity threshold of 0.80, with
all peptides in the same homology group confined to the same fold; each fold was
predicted by a model trained only on its complement, giving every training peptide a
single OOF probability independent of its own optimization [Fu et al.(2012)Fu, Niu,
Zhu, Wu, and Li]. Both experts were optimized with AdamW [Loshchilov and Hut-
ter(2019)Loshchilov, and Hutter]. The fusion weight and decision threshold were se-
lected jointly on the complete group-aware training OOF table by maximizing MCC; in
exact MCC ties, the larger threshold was retained. This procedure yielded 𝑤 1D = 0.72,
𝑤 SE3D = 0.28, and 𝜏 = 0.47022256531284556; these values were then frozen and
applied unchanged to Test1 and Test2. In the final benchmark and public inference
protocol, the conformer availability mask is fixed to zero and the structural probability
is read from the single-structure base logits.

3 RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1 Overall Predictive Performance on the Benchmark Test Sets
    Using the OOF-frozen weight and threshold without dataset-specific retuning, GHX-
Tox correctly classified 1,067 of 1,126 Test1 peptides (TP/TN/FP/FN = 287/780/26/33)
and 551 of 582 strongly imbalanced Test2 peptides (35/516/20/11); the complete metric
set is listed in Table 2. The low Test2 BACC (0.8618) and F1 (0.6931), despite high
accuracy, reflect the difficulty of recovering the 46 toxic peptides and demonstrate why
class-balanced and precision–recall metrics are essential for this benchmark.
    Table 2 also reports previously published models on the same benchmark partitions
(ToxMSRC and ToxPLTC; values transcribed from the original publications, and NR
denotes an unreported metric) [Zhang et al.(2025)Zhang, Ren, and Liang, Liang and
Wang(2026)Liang, and Wang]. GHXTox obtains the highest Test1 ACC, BACC, SP,
MCC, AUROC, and AUPRC among the compared rows, whereas ToxPLTC has higher
Test1 SN; on Test2, GHXTox obtains the highest ACC, SP, MCC, and reported AUPRC,
but its BACC and SN remain below both reference models. This reflects a stronger overall
balance of specificity, correlation, and positive-class ranking rather than comprehensive
superiority on every metric. Because Test1 and Test2 were observed historically, these
differences remain retrospective benchmark evidence and require confirmation on a new
prefrozen external set.
    The final fused probabilities have Brier scores of 0.0410 and 0.0436 and ECE-10
values of 0.0196 and 0.0495 on Test1 and Test2, respectively. The Test2 AUPRC (0.8046)
directly reflects the precision–recall trade-off for the rare toxic class, which is not
conveyed by AUROC alone [Saito and Rehmsmeier(2015)Saito, and Rehmsmeier], and
the 11 false negatives on Test2 delineate the limits of the operating point: high specificity

calibration curves are shown in Figure 2.

3.2 Complementarity of the 1D and 3D Experts
    The two experts are fused only after independent probability generation. The 1D
branch provides the stronger standalone ranking signal, whereas the 3D branch con-
tributes confidence-weighted geometry and local chemical context. This separation
makes the gain attributable to complementary probability evidence rather than dupli-
cated use of an earlier fused prediction (Table 3).
    The experts agreed on most benchmark peptides, while discordant cases revealed
class-asymmetric correction patterns (Figure 3).
    Relative to the calibrated 1D probability stream, the final fusion modestly improves
AUROC/AUPRC and lowers the Brier score on both benchmarks (Table 3), adding
complementary structural ranking information while maintaining a lower probability
error. The structural probability in these rows is the single-structure base output of the
multi-source-trained structural checkpoint; no benchmark conformer cache is activated.
    The 0.72 sequence weight is an empirical result of the training-OOF search, not a
manually imposed assertion that sequence information is intrinsically more important.



                                             17
For the current canonical evaluation, both expert weights and the full-precision deci-
sion threshold of 0.47022256531284556 were frozen from training OOF predictions
and applied without benchmark-specific reweighting, threshold adjustment, or post hoc
selection between equal and unequal fusion. Because Test1 and Test2 had been observed
during earlier project development, this statement describes the formal current protocol
rather than a claim that the benchmarks were historically blinded.
    The experts agreed on most benchmark peptides, but the discordant samples revealed
a reproducible pattern of complementary error correction: the structural expert provided
correct counter-evidence for more than one third of the sequence expert’s errors (Fig-
ure 3B). This rescue was strongly class-asymmetric, with the structural expert mainly
correcting false-positive predictions of the sequence expert, whereas improvements in
toxic-peptide recall were limited. Peptides classified correctly only by the 3D expert
also tended to be shorter, but none of the prespecified attribute comparisons remained
significant after Benjamini–Hochberg correction, so this length pattern is descriptive
rather than evidence that the structural expert is generally superior for short peptides.
These results support a complementary rather than substitutive role for the structural
expert (Figure 3).

3.3 Analysis of the Sequence and Structural Experts
    The 1D expert is not the principal innovation of this work and is analyzed only
briefly. Relative to the earlier pooled-ProtT5–TextCNN pipeline, the LoRA-adapted
ProtT5 with bidirectional Mamba kept BACC and MCC broadly comparable on training
OOF (0.9300 vs 0.9317 and 0.8562 vs 0.8586) while improving the ranking metrics
(AUROC/AUPRC 0.9761/0.9533 vs 0.9724/0.9436), and OOF-fitted Platt calibration
further reduced the Brier score on Test1 and Test2 (Table 4); the stronger ranking stream
benefits the subsequent fusion with structural evidence (architecture in Section 2.3).
    Descriptive structure-quality analysis found an association between mean pLDDT
and subgroup performance in the historical benchmark data. A separate training-OOF
comparison found that hard exclusion of low-confidence samples reduced full-domain
performance, supporting the retained continuous-gating design without establishing
pLDDT as a biological mechanism. In parallel, a C𝛼-level residue graph compresses
chemically complex side chains into single nodes, whereas a paired five-fold OOF ex-
periment showed that adding the full-atom chemical-site residual to the frozen backbone
increased the BACC point estimate from 0.9153 to 0.9171 and the MCC point estimate
from 0.8312 to 0.8342 (Table 7, bottom rows). These modest internal point-estimate
changes are consistent with complementary chemical information but do not by them-
selves establish a general performance gain. Local geometric evidence is meaningful
only in the appropriate sequence context: the same distance or hydrophobic contact can
have different implications depending on residue identity and position, and the model
therefore uses pLDDT-aware cross-attention to let sequence states selectively retrieve
spatial evidence, with node- and peptide-level confidence gating controlling how much
structural context is injected (mechanisms in Section 2.4).
    A single predicted structure provides only one deterministic anchor, whereas short
peptides may exhibit conformational heterogeneity [Abdin and Kim(2024)Abdin, and
Kim]. GHXTox compresses conformer ensembles into statistics and allows them to pro-



                                           18
duce only a zero-initialized logit residual. On training OOF, activating the multi-source
residual increased MCC from 0.87255 to 0.87445 (paired-bootstrap Δ = +0.00190,
95% CI [−0.00043, 0.00454], 𝑝 = 0.203). Under the frozen test protocol, activating
the residual on the covered records changed the metrics negligibly (Tables 5 and 6:
Test1 MCC 0.8705 to 0.8726 and Test2 MCC 0.6673 to 0.6746, with about one pre-
diction flip on each benchmark), which is consistent with the decision not to enable
conformer statistics during benchmark inference. This pattern is consistent with, but
does not establish, a limitation in mapping conformational variation to toxicity labels;
the present ablation cannot isolate that possibility from conformer quality, coverage, or
residual-model capacity.
    After combining all components, the 3D-v2 structural expert reached BACC, MCC,
AUROC, and AUPRC values of 0.9208, 0.8464, 0.9711, and 0.9474 on Test1, compared
with 0.9179, 0.8458, 0.9699, and 0.9426 for the historical C𝛼-only 3D-v1, a consistent
but modest improvement; on Test2, AUPRC increased from 0.6969 to 0.7241 whereas
MCC decreased from 0.6320 to 0.6128 (Table 7 and Figure 4). Finer-grained structural
modeling therefore improves positive-class probability ranking but does not guarantee
simultaneous gains in all metrics at a fixed operating point.
    These ablation and comparison analyses are based on the frozen historical Test1
and Test2 benchmarks and are therefore retrospective evidence. Test2 contains only
46 toxic peptides, so SN, BACC, and MCC are sensitive to a few threshold-adjacent
samples, which partly explains the dataset-dependent direction of the 3D-v2 gains; the
multi-conformer test ablation used the archived test-side conformer cache (486 and 284
covered records on Test1 and Test2, with fixed source and sequence-matching policies)
and activated the residual only on the covered subset, so its negligible effect should not
be extrapolated as evidence that conformer information is useless.

3.4 Robustness, Statistical Uncertainty, and Applicability Analysis
     Point estimates alone are insufficient for distinguishing a stable model improvement
from variation caused by finite samples. We therefore use multi-seed averaging for
the 1D expert and paired, sample-level bootstrap resampling for model comparisons,
all performed on the frozen predictor without retraining, recalibration, or threshold
adjustment. Three full-data 1D models (seeds 42, 123, and 2025) are averaged before
calibration; this ensemble is the final predictor, with no seed selected according to Test1
or Test2 performance, and a 15-member fold-checkpoint ensemble was examined but
not adopted because it did not improve the frozen validation criteria.
     For review-oriented screening, a reject-option policy [Chow(1970)] was defined
using the uncertainty-ranking score given by the decision-boundary proximity 𝑑𝑖 = 1 −
| 𝑝𝑖 − 𝜏|/max(𝜏, 1− 𝜏) with 𝜏 = 0.47022256531284556. On training OOF this proximity
provides an informative ranking: as coverage decreases from 100% to 80%, accepted-
sample error decreases and MCC increases monotonically, whereas equal-count random
rejection shows no comparable improvement; at the prespecified 90% operating point

the cutoff was frozen at 𝑑OOF    = 0.47361765874159856 (Figure 5). Transferring this
cutoff unchanged to Test1 and Test2 reduced the accepted-sample error by about 2.2–2.5
percentage points and improved MCC and BACC, while equal-count random rejection
remained close to the full-coverage error (Table 8). The transfer uses no benchmark-

specific quantile, coverage, class prevalence, or label, so the naturally different realized
coverages reflect a single OOF-derived cutoff rather than retuned abstention rates. This
supports a review-oriented screening workflow in which boundary-proximal predictions
are flagged for review, although it does not constitute independent validation, and the
proximity score is a ranking statistic rather than a calibrated probability of prediction
error. The original binary prediction remains available for every abstained peptide.

3.5 Interpretability and Representative Case Studies
     The interpretation experiments used a prespecified case-selection rule: within each
test set and each expert-outcome group, one toxic and one non-toxic peptide nearest
the fusion threshold were chosen (ties resolved by source index), forming a 16-peptide
cohort. Before perturbation, baseline probabilities were recomputed and compared with
the stored prediction vectors; the maximum absolute discrepancy for the 1D expert
within the case cohort was 3.45 × 10−4 , and the corresponding 3D discrepancies were
5.49 × 10−4 on Test1 and 1.14 × 10−5 on Test2, none of which changed any decision.
The interpretation analyses therefore describe model sensitivity on the frozen predictor
rather than confirmed biological mechanisms.
     Mean pLDDT was strongly associated with the learned global structural gate (Spear-
man 0.960 on Test1, 0.950 on Test2, pooled 0.957), indicating that the model assigns
less structural weight when predicted-structure confidence is lower. This is a model-use
diagnostic and is not direct evidence of peptide flexibility, disorder, or conformational
heterogeneity. Across 445 individual site deletions, most changes were small (median
0.00048, mean 0.00997, 95th percentile 0.057, maximum 0.2994 in 𝑝 3𝐷 ). In the sepa-
rate category-level analysis, all sites carrying a named category were deleted together in
each peptide (121 peptide–category perturbations). The mean absolute changes in 𝑝 3𝐷
were 0.126 for hydrophobic-category deletion, 0.0687 for aromatic-category deletion,
and 0.00179 for sulfur-category deletion. These category-level sensitivities do not de-
scribe individual-site effects and should not be interpreted as causal toxicophores. The
opposing directions of the largest deletion effects (Phe8 increased 𝑝 3𝐷 , Pro19 decreased
it) show that deletion scores depend on the surrounding peptide context and cannot be
read as an intrinsic toxicity label for a functional group (Figure 6).
     Fixed late fusion agreed on most benchmark peptides, and the discordant cases
illustrate its operational consequences (the aggregate pattern is reported in Section 3.2).
The toxic peptide sequence_23_23 (ACLDIGNSCRED, two cysteines) had a weak 1D
probability of 0.2806 but a high-confidence 3D probability of 0.9954; fusion raised the
probability to 0.4808 and restored the correct toxic classification. At the model level, this
example shows that a cysteine-associated structural pattern supplied counter-evidence
to a weak 1D score; it does not establish a cysteine-mediated biological mechanism.
The toxic peptide sequence_34_34 had a correct 1D probability of 0.5447 but a 3D
probability of 3 × 10−5 , and fusion reduced the probability to 0.3922, producing a false
negative; strong sequence evidence can therefore be overridden when the structural
expert supplies sufficiently strong opposing evidence, and such single-sample evidence
conflicts warrant further inspection (Figure 7). These cases illustrate the operational
premise and failure modes of the model: its 1D and 3D scores can agree or conflict, and
fixed late fusion can either correct or introduce an error. They do not confirm a causal



                                             20
biological role for any particular structural feature.

3.6 Limitations and Future Directions
    The most fundamental limitation stems from the single-output nature of three-
dimensional conformation prediction. Short peptides in solution typically populate
multiple interconverting conformations, and toxicity is usually mediated by one partic-
ular conformation or class of conformations; a single predicted structure cannot cover
this multimodal distribution. This is precisely why the project introduced multiple con-
formation generators (PepFlow and AlphaFold2/ColabFold, assigned by length range).
However, different generators exhibit inherent noise, and normalizing conformers across
models and sources further amplifies this inconsistency: activating the multi-conformer
residual on the test sets produced negligible and inconsistent effects that did not justify
a public inference protocol, and source-independent and fusion-aware candidates also
failed their prespecified retention gates (Section 3.3, Tables 5 and 6), so the conformer
residual was eventually disabled for benchmark and public inference and the structural
information-extraction capacity of the model was not fully released. On training OOF the
residual improved MCC by only +0.00190 (𝑝 = 0.203, not significant), and expanding
the conformer cohort from 250 to 761 peptides did not pass the retention gates because
the added records were all negative. In addition, full-atom conformational sampling
(e.g., PepFlow) is computationally far more expensive than single-structure prediction,
which limited large-scale generation and deployment. We therefore hope that next-
generation protein structure prediction models can provide, at acceptable computational
cost, multiple possible conformations and their probabilities (for example, lightweight
multi-conformer sampling based on flow matching or diffusion), more closely matching
the multimodal nature of peptide conformation and potentially further releasing the
value of conformational information in toxicity prediction.
    The second limitation is that the 1D sequence and 3D conformation dimensions are
combined with a fixed 0.72/0.28 weight rather than dynamically adjusted per sample.
This may partly stem from the first limitation: the inherent noise among different
conformation predictors masks the information that would indicate which dimension
should be trusted for a given sample, and when structural evidence is itself inconsistent
across generators, the model cannot reliably distinguish, per sample, the reliability
of sequence evidence from that of conformational evidence and therefore falls back
to fixed weights. Consistent with this, sample-level confidence gates and dynamic
routing attempts did not pass the retention gates: the former produced MCC values
below the fixed default, and the latter improved ranking and calibration but did not
exceed the frozen default’s decision metrics (Section 3.4). This reflects that the two
dimensions have not yet achieved sufficiently effective information complementarity;
adaptive and interpretable fusion based on evidence reliability, together with more
consistent conformational evidence sources, are directions for future work.

4 CONCLUSIONS
    GHXTox explores the complementary joint use of sequence and conformational
evidence by combining a task-adaptive LoRA-ProtT5–Bi-Mamba sequence expert with
a pLDDT-aware multi-scale structural expert through probability-level fusion; the two



                                             21
experts are independent and their complementarity is characterized by the structural
expert correcting a subset of sequence errors in a class-asymmetric way (Section 3.2).
The structural expert integrates residue geometry, full-atom-derived chemical sites,
and sequence-conditioned structural evidence. A separate multi-conformer residual
was trained with the single-structure backbone frozen and is retained only for controlled
ablations. The final predictor disables that residual and uses the structural expert’s single-
structure base probability. The 1D/3D weights (0.72/0.28) and the decision threshold
(0.47022256531284556) were frozen from training OOF predictions and applied in the
current evaluation without benchmark-specific retuning; Test1 and Test2 are historical
benchmarks, not newly blinded external sets.
    On the frozen historical Test1 and Test2 benchmarks, the model reaches MCC values
of 0.8705 and 0.6673 and AUPRC values of 0.9674 and 0.8046, and the final fusion
improves AUROC, AUPRC, and the Brier score relative to the calibrated 1D stream.
The 3D expert correctly classified 36.92% and 37.50% of the respective Test1 and Test2
errors made by the 1D expert, with most of these structural rescues occurring among non-
toxic peptides. An OOF-frozen selective policy further reduced accepted-sample error
to 2.71% and 3.16% at realized coverages of 88.54% and 86.94%, respectively, with-
out benchmark-specific cutoff selection. Conformer-derived information produced only
modest and non-uniform improvements, indicating that mapping conformational varia-
tion to toxicity-relevant evidence remains an important limitation. The interpretability
analyses describe model sensitivity rather than causal mechanisms.
    Several limitations remain, most notably the single-output nature of conformation
prediction and the fixed probability-level fusion between the two dimensions, as dis-
cussed in Section 3.6; both benchmarks were also observed during earlier model devel-
opment, and the structures are predicted rather than experimental. Looking ahead, we
hope that more powerful three-dimensional conformation models, capable of produc-
ing multiple conformations with their probabilities at acceptable computational cost,
together with dynamic, evidence-reliability-aware fusion between the sequence and
structural dimensions, will enable a deeper complementary joint use of the two types of
information, with experimental validation where feasible.
