GHXTox: Peptide toxicity prediction by fusing task-adaptive
 protein language model with pLDDT-aware multi-scale 3D
                   structural modeling
               Liuyu Xu𝑎 , Kehan Gao𝑎 , Lechen Hao𝑎 , Shengli Zhang𝑎,∗
𝑎 School of Mathematics and Statistics, Xidian University, Xi’an 710071, P. R. China

Abstract. Peptide toxicity is a major safety concern in peptide-based drug development,
motivating accurate computational screening methods. Existing methods have begun
to explore multi-dimensional (multimodal) feature fusion and toxicity prediction based
on the three-dimensional (3D) conformations of peptides, yet most of them still un-
derutilize 3D structural information, and systematic multi-dimensional fusion remains
rare. Here, we propose GHXTox, a hierarchical dual-level fusion framework in which a
3D structural expert first integrates sequence semantics with spatial structural features
through predicted local-distance difference test (pLDDT)-aware cross-attention, and is
then fused at the probability level with task-adaptive one-dimensional (1D) sequence
expert using weights frozen on training out-of-fold (OOF) predictions. The 1D branch
analyzes sequences with a protein language model (ProtT5 fine-tuned with low-rank
adaptation, LoRA) and a bidirectional Mamba state-space model. The 3D expert inte-
grates an ESMFold-derived structural anchor, pLDDT-aware residue geometry, and full-
atom-derived chemical sites. A separate residual head was trained on multi-conformer
descriptors while the single-structure backbone was frozen. This residual is disabled
in the canonical predictor and is reported only in controlled ablations; benchmark and
public inference use the single-structure base logits. On the frozen historical benchmark
test sets, GHXTox achieves MCC values of 0.8705 and 0.6673 and AUPRC values of
0.9674 and 0.8046 on test set 1 and test set 2, respectively. Further analyses characterize
residue-level sequence sensitivity, confidence–gate associations, chemical-site pertur-
bations, expert disagreement, and an OOF-frozen selective-prediction rule. At realized
coverages of 88.54% and 86.94%, the selective rule reduced the accepted-sample error
to 2.71% and 3.16% on test set 1 and test set 2 without benchmark-specific cutoff selec-
tion. These findings are interpreted as model-level evidence rather than causal biological
mechanisms. Frozen-model analyses indicate that the conformer contribution is small
and non-uniform: under the structural expert, substantial geometric variation does not
necessarily translate into stable outcome-aligned probability variation.
Keywords: peptide toxicity prediction; multimodal deep learning; protein language
model; bidirectional Mamba; pLDDT-aware multi-scale 3D modeling; interpretability

1 INTRODUCTION
    Peptides are increasingly used as therapeutic candidates because of their high target
specificity and functional diversity [26]. However, peptide toxicity remains a major
safety concern during drug development, and the accurate and efficient identification
of toxic peptides is a major bottleneck in peptide-based drug discovery [39]. Because
conventional experimental toxicity assays are costly, time-consuming, and difficult to
scale, reliable computational predictors are highly desirable [39].
    Computational peptide toxicity prediction has evolved from traditional machine
learning methods based on handcrafted physicochemical features toward deep learn-
ing approaches with stronger representation-learning capability. Current mainstream
methods can be roughly grouped into two categories. The first category operates on
sequence-level (one-dimensional, 1D) and/or atomic-level (two-dimensional, 2D) rep-
resentations. For example, ToxMSRC combines continuous bag-of-words (CBOW) em-
beddings, multi-scale convolutional neural networks, and a bidirectional long short-term
memory network with residual connections, achieving strong performance on the bench-
mark dataset [39]. ToxPLTC integrates pooled ProtT5 embeddings, borderline-SMOTE
augmentation, and a text convolutional neural network [20]. ToxiPep fuses context-
aware sequence representations with atomic-level graphs through a cross-attention
mechanism [11]. HyPepTox-Fuse further combines embeddings from multiple protein
language models with conventional descriptors [34]. Although these models achieve
strong performance within their respective modalities, they largely neglect the three-
dimensional (3D) conformations of peptides, which are closely related to toxicity;
cross-dimensional information interaction and mutual validation remain limited, and
their interpretability is mostly confined to the sequence level.
    The second category attempts to exploit 3D structural information. PeptiTox uses
ESM2 sequence embeddings together with ESMFold-predicted 3D structures and ge-
ometric graph neural networks [36]. StrucToxNet feeds ProtT5 sequence embeddings
and ESMFold-predicted structures into an equivariant graph neural network (EGNN)
and systematically analyzes structural quality [16]. These studies demonstrate the value
of 3D conformations for toxicity prediction. However, these predictors still depend on
predicted structures whose reliability can vary for short peptides, and both evaluate one
predicted conformation per sequence rather than an explicit conformer ensemble. Exist-
ing designs use predicted-structure confidence primarily for filtering or structural-quality
analysis rather than as a continuous message-level reliability signal [12, 16, 36]. More-
over, the fusion between the sequence and structure branches remains relatively shallow,
and no systematic multi-dimensional fusion that combines task-adaptive sequence model
with confidence-aware multi-scale structure modeling has been established.
    To address these limitations, we propose GHXTox, an interpretable hierarchical dual-
level fusion framework for peptide toxicity prediction. Task-adaptive 1D sequence expert
combines a ProtT5 protein language model, adapted by low-rank adaptation (LoRA),
with a bidirectional Mamba state-space encoder and three-seed ensembling [7, 10, 15].
