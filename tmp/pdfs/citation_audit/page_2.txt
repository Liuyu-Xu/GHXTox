1D) and/or atomic-level (two-dimensional, 2D) representations. For example, ToxMSRC com-
bines continuous bag-of-words (CBOW) embeddings, multi-scale convolutional neural networks,
and a bidirectional long short-term memory network with residual connections, achieving strong
performance on the benchmark dataset [2]. ToxPLTC integrates pooled ProtT5 embeddings,
borderline-SMOTE augmentation, and a text convolutional neural network [3]. ToxiPep fuses
context-aware sequence representations with atomic-level graphs through a cross-attention mech-
anism [4]. HyPepTox-Fuse further combines embeddings from multiple protein language models
with conventional descriptors [5]. Although these models achieve strong performance within
their respective modalities, they largely neglect the three-dimensional (3D) conformations of
peptides, which are closely related to toxicity; cross-dimensional information interaction and
mutual validation remain limited, and their interpretability is mostly confined to the sequence
level.
     The second category attempts to exploit 3D structural information. PeptiTox uses ESM2 se-
quence embeddings together with ESMFold-predicted 3D structures and geometric graph neural
networks [6]. StrucToxNet feeds ProtT5 sequence embeddings and ESMFold-predicted struc-
tures into an equivariant graph neural network (EGNN) and systematically analyzes structural
quality [7]. These studies demonstrate the value of 3D conformations for toxicity prediction.
However, these predictors still depend on predicted structures whose reliability can vary for short
peptides, and both evaluate one predicted conformation per sequence rather than an explicit
conformer ensemble. Existing designs use predicted-structure confidence primarily for filtering
or structural-quality analysis rather than as a continuous message-level reliability signal [6–8].
Moreover, the fusion between the sequence and structure branches remains relatively shallow,
and no systematic multi-dimensional fusion that combines a task-adaptive sequence model with
confidence-aware multi-scale structure modeling has been established.
     To address these limitations, we propose GHXTox, an interpretable hierarchical dual-level
fusion framework for peptide toxicity prediction. A task-adaptive 1D sequence expert combines
a ProtT5 protein language model, adapted by low-rank adaptation (LoRA), with a bidirectional
Mamba state-space encoder and three-seed ensembling [9–11]. A sequence-enhanced 3D struc-
tural expert integrates sequence semantics with spatial structure through pLDDT-aware cross-
attention, ESMFold-derived geometry, full-atom chemical sites, and confidence gating across
multiple structural scales; PepFlow and AlphaFold2/ColabFold conformer ensembles were used
to train and evaluate a separate residual head while the single-structure backbone remained
frozen; this head is retained only for controlled ablations, and the final predictor uses the single-
structure ESMFold anchor [12–15]. The two expert probabilities are fused at the probability
level with weights fixed on training out-of-fold (OOF) predictions; the 1D probabilities are first
calibrated on the same OOF table, and no benchmark labels are used during this selection.
Gated sequence–structure cross-attention inside the structural expert further informs the inter-
action design [16]. Ablation experiments show that the contribution of structural evidence is
dataset- and metric-dependent: fusion improves several fixed-threshold and probability-quality
measures on Test1 while maintaining broadly comparable performance on Test2. Post hoc anal-
yses characterize residue-level sensitivity, confidence gating, chemical-site perturbations, and
expert disagreement without assigning causal biological meaning to these signals. The frame-
work is evaluated on the frozen historical benchmarks described in Section 2.1.
     In summary, the main contributions of this work are threefold: (1) we propose GHXTox,
an interpretable hierarchical dual-level fusion framework that explores the complementary joint
use of one-dimensional sequence and three-dimensional conformational information, integrating a
task-adaptive 1D protein language model with pLDDT-aware multi-scale 3D structure modeling
and combining the two experts at the probability level (Sections 2.5 and 3.2); (2) the 3D branch
