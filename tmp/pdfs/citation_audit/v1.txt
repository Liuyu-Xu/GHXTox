    GHXTox: Peptide toxicity prediction by fusing a task-adaptive
     protein language model with pLDDT-aware multi-scale 3D
                       structural modeling



                                              Abstract
          Peptide toxicity is a major safety concern in peptide-based drug development, moti-
      vating accurate computational screening methods. Existing methods have begun to ex-
      plore multi-dimensional (multimodal) feature fusion and toxicity prediction based on the
      three-dimensional (3D) conformations of peptides, yet most of them still underutilize 3D
      structural information, and systematic multi-dimensional fusion remains rare. Here, we pro-
      pose GHXTox, a hierarchical dual-level fusion framework in which a 3D structural expert
      first integrates sequence semantics with spatial structural features through predicted local-
      distance difference test (pLDDT)-aware cross-attention, and is then fused at the probability
      level with a task-adaptive one-dimensional (1D) sequence expert using weights frozen on
      training out-of-fold (OOF) predictions. The 1D branch analyzes sequences with a protein
      language model (ProtT5 fine-tuned with low-rank adaptation, LoRA) and a bidirectional
      Mamba state-space model. The 3D expert integrates an ESMFold-derived structural anchor,
      pLDDT-aware residue geometry, and full-atom-derived chemical sites. A separate residual
      head was trained on multi-conformer descriptors while the single-structure backbone was
      frozen. This residual is disabled in the canonical predictor and is reported only in con-
      trolled ablations; benchmark and public inference use the single-structure base logits. On
      the frozen historical benchmark test sets, GHXTox achieves MCC values of 0.8705 and 0.6673
      and AUPRC values of 0.9674 and 0.8046 on Test1 and Test2, respectively. Further analy-
      ses characterize residue-level sequence sensitivity, confidence–gate associations, chemical-site
      perturbations, expert disagreement, and an OOF-frozen selective-prediction rule. At real-
      ized coverages of 88.54% and 86.94%, the selective rule reduced the accepted-sample error
      to 2.71% and 3.16% on Test1 and Test2 without benchmark-specific cutoff selection. These
      findings are interpreted as model-level evidence rather than causal biological mechanisms.
      Frozen-model analyses indicate that the conformer contribution is small and non-uniform:
      under the structural expert, substantial geometric variation does not necessarily translate
      into stable outcome-aligned probability variation.

Keywords: peptide toxicity prediction; multimodal deep learning; protein language model;
bidirectional Mamba; pLDDT-aware multi-scale 3D modeling; interpretability


1     INTRODUCTION
      Peptides are increasingly used as therapeutic candidates because of their high target speci-
ficity and functional diversity [1]. However, peptide toxicity remains a major safety concern
during drug development, and the accurate and efficient identification of toxic peptides is a ma-
jor bottleneck in peptide-based drug discovery [2]. Because conventional experimental toxicity
assays are costly, time-consuming, and difficult to scale, reliable computational predictors are
highly desirable [2].
      Computational peptide toxicity prediction has evolved from traditional machine learn-
ing methods based on handcrafted physicochemical features toward deep learning approaches
with stronger representation-learning capability. Current mainstream methods can be roughly
grouped into two categories. The first category operates on sequence-level (one-dimensional,

                                                     1
1D) and/or atomic-level (two-dimensional, 2D) representations. For example, ToxMSRC com-
bines continuous bag-of-words (CBOW) embeddings, multi-scale convolutional neural networks,
and a bidirectional long short-term memory network with residual connections, achieving strong
performance on the benchmark dataset [2]. ToxPLTC integrates pooled ProtT5 embeddings,
borderline-SMOTE augmentation, and a text convolutional neural network [3]. ToxiPep fuses
context-aware sequence representations with atomic-level graphs through a cross-attention mech-
anism [4]. HyPepTox-Fuse further combines embeddings from multiple protein language models
with conventional descriptors [5]. Although these models achieve strong performance within
their respective modalities, they largely neglect the three-dimensional (3D) conformations of
peptides, which are closely related to toxicity; cross-dimensional information interaction and
mutual validation remain limited, and their interpretability is mostly confined to the sequence
level.
     The second category attempts to exploit 3D structural information. PeptiTox uses ESM2 se-
quence embeddings together with ESMFold-predicted 3D structures and geometric graph neural
networks [6]. StrucToxNet feeds ProtT5 sequence embeddings and ESMFold-predicted struc-
tures into an equivariant graph neural network (EGNN) and systematically analyzes structural
quality [7]. These studies demonstrate the value of 3D conformations for toxicity prediction.
However, these predictors still depend on predicted structures whose reliability can vary for short
peptides, and both evaluate one predicted conformation per sequence rather than an explicit
conformer ensemble. Existing designs use predicted-structure confidence primarily for filtering
or structural-quality analysis rather than as a continuous message-level reliability signal [6–8].
Moreover, the fusion between the sequence and structure branches remains relatively shallow,
and no systematic multi-dimensional fusion that combines a task-adaptive sequence model with
confidence-aware multi-scale structure modeling has been established.
     To address these limitations, we propose GHXTox, an interpretable hierarchical dual-level
fusion framework for peptide toxicity prediction. A task-adaptive 1D sequence expert combines
a ProtT5 protein language model, adapted by low-rank adaptation (LoRA), with a bidirectional
Mamba state-space encoder and three-seed ensembling [9–11]. A sequence-enhanced 3D struc-
tural expert integrates sequence semantics with spatial structure through pLDDT-aware cross-
attention, ESMFold-derived geometry, full-atom chemical sites, and confidence gating across
multiple structural scales; PepFlow and AlphaFold2/ColabFold conformer ensembles were used
to train and evaluate a separate residual head while the single-structure backbone remained
frozen; this head is retained only for controlled ablations, and the final predictor uses the single-
structure ESMFold anchor [12–15]. The two expert probabilities are fused at the probability
level with weights fixed on training out-of-fold (OOF) predictions; the 1D probabilities are first
calibrated on the same OOF table, and no benchmark labels are used during this selection.
Gated sequence–structure cross-attention inside the structural expert further informs the inter-
action design [16]. Ablation experiments show that the contribution of structural evidence is
dataset- and metric-dependent: fusion improves several fixed-threshold and probability-quality
measures on Test1 while maintaining broadly comparable performance on Test2. Post hoc anal-
yses characterize residue-level sensitivity, confidence gating, chemical-site perturbations, and
expert disagreement without assigning causal biological meaning to these signals. The frame-
work is evaluated on the frozen historical benchmarks described in Section 2.1.
     In summary, the main contributions of this work are threefold: (1) we propose GHXTox,
an interpretable hierarchical dual-level fusion framework that explores the complementary joint
use of one-dimensional sequence and three-dimensional conformational information, integrating a
task-adaptive 1D protein language model with pLDDT-aware multi-scale 3D structure modeling
and combining the two experts at the probability level (Sections 2.5 and 3.2); (2) the 3D branch
designs a multi-scale structural information extraction strategy spanning from local residue
functional groups to the whole peptide, and incorporates pLDDT confidence into structural
feature processing through continuous down-weighting and dynamic gating; and (3) extensive

experiments on the frozen historical benchmark test sets show that GHXTox improves several
fixed-threshold and probability-quality measures on Test1 while maintaining broadly comparable
performance on Test2, while ablation, robustness, and interpretability analyses characterize the
conditions under which sequence and structural evidence are complementary.


2     MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1   Datasets
      We evaluate GHXTox on the benchmark datasets constructed by CAPTP [17] and subse-
quently adopted by ToxMSRC [2]. Following the data construction protocol of ToxMSRC [2],
toxic peptide sequences were merged from three databases, namely CSM-Toxin [18], Toxin-
Pred2 [19], and ATSE [20], and sequences longer than 50 residues were removed. Non-toxic
sequences were retrieved from UniProt [21] using the query “NOT KW-0800 AND NOT KW-
0020 AND reviewed:true”, and sequences containing non-standard amino acids or exceeding
50 residues were excluded. After combining the two groups and removing samples with con-
flicting labels, CD-HIT [22] was applied with a sequence-identity threshold of 0.9 to eliminate
redundant sequences, yielding 2,138 toxic and 5,375 non-toxic peptides. Following the same
partition as ToxMSRC [2], we used 85% of the data as the training set and the remaining
15% as the benchmark Test1 set, resulting in 1,818 toxic and 4,569 non-toxic training peptides
and 320 toxic and 806 non-toxic Test1 peptides. An additional benchmark Test2 set, contain-
ing 46 toxic and 536 non-toxic peptides, is used for frozen historical evaluation. Both Test1
and Test2 had been observed during historical project development and are therefore treated
as frozen historical benchmarks rather than newly blinded external validation sets; their la-
bels were not used in the formal OOF calibration, fusion-weight selection, or threshold-freezing
procedure. ToxinPred 3.0 [23] was consulted as an external method and data-source reference
during project development, but its records were not added to the present training set or to the
formal Test1/Test2 result tables. Table 1 summarizes the statistics of the datasets used in this
study.

2.2   Overview of the Model
     GHXTox is a hierarchical dual-level fusion framework for peptide toxicity prediction: a task-
adaptive one-dimensional (1D) sequence expert and a sequence-enhanced three-dimensional (3D)
structural expert generate independent probabilities, interact internally at the feature level, and
are combined at the probability level.
     The 1D expert processes the peptide sequence with a LoRA-adapted ProtT5 encoder [9,10]
followed by a bidirectional Mamba state-space model [11], averages three seed models, and
applies OOF-fitted Platt calibration to produce p1D (Section 2.3). The 3D expert integrates
sequence semantics, residue geometry, full-atom chemical sites, and pLDDT confidence across
residue, local-spatial, chemical-site, and peptide scales, combines sequence and spatial states
through pLDDT-aware cross-attention, and emits pSE3D (Section 2.4).
     The two probabilities are combined at the probability level; the fusion rule, the decision
threshold, and the final output are described in Section 2.5.

2.3    Task-Adaptive 1D Sequence Modeling
     The 1D sequence expert learns toxicity-related contextual semantics directly from peptide
sequences. Instead of reducing a frozen protein-language-model output to a fixed mean vector,
GHXTox preserves the residue-level representations of ProtT5, adapts the pretrained encoder
to the toxicity task through low-rank adaptation (LoRA), models residue dependencies with
a bidirectional Mamba encoder, and aggregates the residue states by masked mean pooling
before classification. Three full-data models with different random seeds are combined by equal-
weight probability averaging followed by Platt calibration fitted exclusively on training out-of-
fold (OOF) predictions.
     Each residue is first written in the space-separated format expected by the ProtT5 tokenizer.
A binary residue-validity mask is constructed from the raw residue count, applied to invalid po-
sitions before each selective state-space scan, and reused in the final pooling; canonical inference
follows the fixed batch-construction procedure used to generate the reported predictions. The
tokenized sequence is processed by the ProtT5-XL-UniRef50 encoder [9] with LoRA, yielding
residue-level representations that retain position-specific contextual semantics. To adapt this
general-purpose encoder to the toxicity task, LoRA [10] is applied to all of its linear layers with
rank r = 8, scaling α = 16, and dropout 0.1: the pretrained weights stay frozen, and only the
zero-initialized low-rank update (W = W0 + αr BA) and the downstream modules are train-
able. The 1024-dimensional representations are then projected into the 192-dimensional Mamba
hidden space through a layer-norm–GELU–dropout block.
     Two bidirectional Mamba layers [11] are then stacked after the projection block to capture
the sequential dependencies relevant to peptide toxicity. Each directional block follows the stan-
dard selective state-space formulation with input-dependent discretization and a 16-dimensional
recurrent state; forward and backward scans are applied to the masked sequence, and their out-
puts are concatenated, fused by a linear layer, and added through a residual connection, so every
residue is contextualized by both its N-terminal and C-terminal contexts. Invalid positions are


                                                         4
masked before the selective scan and excluded from masked pooling; because the depthwise con-
volution precedes this scan mask in the implementation, the reported inference protocol retains
the canonical batch construction. The resulting residue states are aggregated by masked mean
pooling over the valid positions, and a layer-normalized single-output linear head yields the un-
calibrated toxicity probability. Because non-toxic peptides far outnumber toxic peptides in the
training data, a positive-class-weighted binary cross-entropy loss is used instead of SMOTE or
Borderline-SMOTE, with the positive-class weight w+ = N− /N+ recomputed from each fold’s
training partition; this emphasizes the minority class without generating synthetic samples or
leaking held-out information.
      To reduce the variance of a single training run, three 1D models are trained under identi-
cal settings with random seeds 42, 123, and 2025, and the ensemble score is the equal-weight
arithmetic average of the three full-data models; no seed is selected on test performance and no
seed-specific weight is introduced. Although averaging reduces variance, neural-network proba-
bilities may remain miscalibrated, so a monotonic two-parameter Platt calibrator [24] is fitted
to the training OOF scores (generated with the fixed group-aware five-fold partition, Section
2.5) and applied to the three-seed average at inference. Because the calibration is monotonic, it
preserves the sample ranking. Test1/Test2 labels are not used for member selection, calibration
fitting, or threshold optimization. The calibrated probability p1D is the sequence-expert output
used in the top-level fusion (Section 2.5); no additional calibrator is fitted after fusion.

2.4   pLDDT-Aware Multi-Scale 3D Structural Modeling
     Peptide toxicity is influenced not only by amino acid composition and linear residue order
but also by the three-dimensional (3D) arrangement of charged groups, hydrophobic regions,
aromatic systems, and sulfur-containing moieties. Because peptides are generally short, con-
formationally flexible, and poorly covered by experimentally determined structures, treating
a single predicted conformation as an error-free structural observation may amplify structure-
prediction errors, whereas Cα-only or peptide-level summary representations fail to capture the
local spatial-chemical environments that are relevant to toxicity [6–8].
     To address these limitations, GHXTox employs a pLDDT-aware multi-scale structural ex-
pert that captures complementary single-structure information at four levels: residue semantics,
spatial residue neighborhoods, full-atom chemical sites, and peptide-level global properties (Fig-
ure 2). A separate availability-masked conformer-ensemble residual is retained as an optional
ablation branch but is not part of the canonical predictor. Sections 2.4.1–2.4.6 describe, in
turn, single-structure prediction and conformer preparation, residue feature construction and
the hybrid geometric graph, pLDDT-aware equivariant graph encoding, full-atom chemical-
site message passing with the availability-masked conformer-ensemble logit residual, sequence–
structure cross-attention, and global feature integration with classification. Throughout, pLDDT

                                                   5
is treated as a continuous reliability signal rather than a hard filter: low-confidence structural
evidence is down-weighted in geometric edge weighting, chemical-site messages, and residue- and
peptide-level structural gating instead of being removed, preserving structural coverage while
limiting the influence of uncertain coordinates. pLDDT is interpreted here as a local confidence
estimate produced by the structure-prediction model, not as an experimentally determined mea-
sure of structural accuracy, conformational flexibility, or biological activity.

2.4.1    Peptide Structure Prediction and Conformer Preparation
     For a peptide sequence of length L, S = (a1 , a2 , . . . , aL ), ESMFold is first used to pre-
dict its three-dimensional structure [12]. The predicted structure is stored as a full-atom
PDB file, from which residue-level Cα coordinates, backbone coordinates, full-atom-derived
chemical-site coordinates, and pLDDT scores are extracted. The Cα coordinates are collected
as X = [x1 , . . . , xL ]⊤ ∈ RL×3 , A denotes the full-atom-PDB-derived backbone and chemical-site
geometry, and q = (q1 , . . . , qL ) ∈ [0, 1]L contains the residue-level pLDDT confidence scores
rescaled to [0, 1], where qi denotes the residue-level pLDDT score of residue i. The Cα coordi-
nates serve as the primary spatial variables of the residue-level equivariant graph, whereas the
full-atom coordinates are used to construct local chemical sites. Before geometric encoding, the

                                                   6
coordinates are centered at the peptide centroid to remove the effect of absolute translation.
     A single predicted structure provides a common structural anchor but cannot represent
potential conformational heterogeneity. The conformer-residual stage was therefore trained,
with the single-structure spatial backbone, chemical-site branch, and base classifier frozen, on a
multi-source conformer variant using a 2,795-peptide cohort with up to eight validated, aligned
conformers per peptide. PepFlow is a generalized Boltzmann generator that performs direct
all-atom conformational-ensemble sampling from the peptide energy landscape rather than pre-
dicting a single structure; it has been shown to efficiently recapitulate experimental ensembles
of short linear motifs (SLiMs, typically 3–15 residues) and to generate all-atom conformations
for peptides of different lengths [15]. Short-peptide conformations are therefore provided by
PepFlow. In our pipeline, up to eight conformations are retained per input peptide, and
PepFlow is applied to peptides of at most 15 residues (the actual training cohort spans 11–
15 residues and comprises 761 records with eight conformers each). Longer peptides are pro-
vided by AlphaFold2/ColabFold: AlphaFold2 is a high-accuracy single-structure predictor [13],
and its peptide-structure performance has been systematically evaluated for peptides of 16–60
residues [8], an interval that covers the 16–30-residue cohort; ColabFold, an MSA-accelerated
AlphaFold2 implementation [14], is run in our pipeline with five models and multiple seeds to
generate candidate conformations. These candidates are filtered by an average-pLDDT quality
floor and then reduced to eight diverse conformers per peptide by farthest-point sampling on
Kabsch-aligned Cα-RMSD [25], yielding 2,034 training records. These sources are represented
explicitly in the residual head; nevertheless, generator identity remains partially coupled to
peptide length and is treated as a limitation rather than as an independently identified causal
factor.
     For controlled ablation only, a test-side conformer cache was constructed with fixed source
and sequence-matching policies without using Test1 or Test2 labels; these caches are not used
by the final benchmark predictor or the public inference interface. To remove arbitrary rigid-
body differences and make the statistics comparable across peptides, every admitted ensemble
is normalized before being consumed by the residual branch. An ensemble is represented as

so all conformers share the same residue-index order without reordering or truncation. Second,
taking the first conformer as the reference, every conformer is centered and aligned to the
reference frame by Kabsch alignment, which obtains an optimal rotation by singular value
decomposition with reflection correction, yielding the aligned coordinates and the per-residue
fluctuation:

inference from the absence of conformers and simply falls back to the single-structure prediction.

mined trajectories or rigorous thermodynamic ensembles. Consequently, RMSF, contact occu-
pancy, and distance variance are interpreted as sampling-derived descriptors of conformational
variation rather than direct measurements of peptide dynamics in solution.

fluence representation learning at the propagation stage. Low pLDDT does not imply that a
residue is biologically unimportant; it may reflect prediction error or genuine conformational
flexibility, so the model reduces the reliability of uncertain coordinate evidence while preserving
the residue’s sequence-semantic and physicochemical features.

2.4.4   Full-Atom Chemical-Site Message Passing
     A Cα-based residue graph captures the global fold and spatial neighborhoods but cannot
resolve chemically distinct moieties within the same side chain. For example, tyrosine contains
both an aromatic ring and a hydroxyl group, whereas tryptophan contains an aromatic system
and an indole nitrogen. Compressing each side chain into a single centroid would mix these
chemically different moieties. GHXTox therefore extracts chemistry-aware pseudo-sites from the
predicted full-atom PDB structure and processes them with a dedicated local message-passing
branch [28].
     To retain this chemical detail, up to two chemical sites are kept for each residue. Site identity
is represented by an eight-dimensional multi-hot vector tir ∈ {0, 1}8 allowing overlapping types:
positive, negative, donor, acceptor, aromatic, hydrophobic, sulfur, and conformational, where r
indexes the chemical site within residue i. Multiple types may be assigned to the same site: for
example, the cysteine sulfur site is labeled as donor, hydrophobic, and sulfur-containing, whereas
the proline ring is labeled as hydrophobic and conformationally constrained; the aromatic ring
and hydroxyl group of tyrosine are represented as separate sites, as are the aromatic system and
the indole NE1 nitrogen (donor) of tryptophan.
     Each site is further localized and oriented in the predicted structure: its coordinate is the
mean position of the heavy atoms defining the corresponding chemical moiety, and its orientation
is represented by either a bond direction or an unsigned plane normal. Because the sign of a plane
normal is arbitrary, downstream directional features use absolute cosines, preserving rotation
invariance and eliminating orientation-sign ambiguity. Combining these attributes, the initial

2.5   Fusion and Final Output
    The 1D expert emits the OOF-calibrated probability p1D (Section 2.3), and the structural
expert emits pSE3D (Section 2.4). The two probabilities are fused linearly in probability space
with weights frozen on training out-of-fold (OOF) predictions:

                                pGHXTox = 0.72 p1D + 0.28 pSE3D ,                                      (17)

The fused probability pGHXTox is the model’s continuous toxicity-probability output; it is
binarized directly at the OOF-frozen threshold, predicting toxic (ŷ = 1) if pGHXTox ≥
0.47022256531284556 and non-toxic (ŷ = 0) otherwise. The default output is therefore
(pGHXTox , ŷ). Experiments showed that directly emitting the fused probability yields the best
overall performance, so no additional network or calibration is applied after fusion.
      The two experts were trained independently because they differ in input representation,
parameter scale, and optimization behavior (the 1D and 3D loss, calibration, and training details
are described in Sections 2.3 and 2.4). Group-aware training OOF predictions were used for
calibration, fusion-weight selection, and threshold determination, and Test1/Test2 labels were
excluded from all of these procedures. The training sequences were clustered by CD-HIT at
a sequence-identity threshold of 0.80, with all peptides in the same homology group confined
to the same fold; each fold was predicted by a model trained only on its complement, giving
every training peptide a single OOF probability independent of its own optimization [22]. Both
experts were optimized with AdamW [30]. The fusion weight and decision threshold were selected
jointly on the complete group-aware training OOF table by maximizing MCC; in exact MCC
ties, the larger threshold was retained. This procedure yielded w1D = 0.72, wSE3D = 0.28, and
τ = 0.47022256531284556; these values were then frozen and applied unchanged to Test1 and
Test2. In the final benchmark and public inference protocol, the conformer availability mask is
fixed to zero and the structural probability is read from the single-structure base logits.

3     RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1   Overall Predictive Performance on the Benchmark Test Sets
      Using the OOF-frozen weight and threshold without dataset-specific retuning, GHXTox
correctly classified 1,067 of 1,126 Test1 peptides (TP/TN/FP/FN = 287/780/26/33) and 551
of 582 strongly imbalanced Test2 peptides (35/516/20/11); the complete metric set is listed
in Table 2. The low Test2 BACC (0.8618) and F1 (0.6931), despite high accuracy, reflect the
difficulty of recovering the 46 toxic peptides and demonstrate why class-balanced and precision–
recall metrics are essential for this benchmark.
      Table 2 also reports previously published models on the same benchmark partitions (ToxM-
SRC and ToxPLTC; values transcribed from the original publications, and NR denotes an unre-
ported metric) [2, 3]. GHXTox obtains the highest Test1 ACC, BACC, SP, MCC, AUROC, and
AUPRC among the compared rows, whereas ToxPLTC has higher Test1 SN; on Test2, GHXTox
obtains the highest ACC, SP, MCC, and reported AUPRC, but its BACC and SN remain below
both reference models. This reflects a stronger overall balance of specificity, correlation, and
positive-class ranking rather than comprehensive superiority on every metric. Because Test1
and Test2 were observed historically, these differences remain retrospective benchmark evidence
and require confirmation on a new prefrozen external set.
      The final fused probabilities have Brier scores of 0.0410 and 0.0436 and ECE-10 values
of 0.0196 and 0.0495 on Test1 and Test2, respectively. The Test2 AUPRC (0.8046) directly
reflects the precision–recall trade-off for the rare toxic class, which is not conveyed by AUROC
alone [31], and the 11 false negatives on Test2 delineate the limits of the operating point: high
specificity should not be interpreted as complete minority-class coverage. Discrimination and
calibration curves are shown in Figure 3.

activating the multi-source residual increased MCC from 0.87255 to 0.87445 (paired-bootstrap

each benchmark), which is consistent with the decision not to enable conformer statistics during
benchmark inference. This pattern is consistent with, but does not establish, a limitation in
mapping conformational variation to toxicity labels; the present ablation cannot isolate that
possibility from conformer quality, coverage, or residual-model capacity.

3.4      Robustness, Statistical Uncertainty, and Applicability Analysis
     Point estimates alone are insufficient for distinguishing a stable model improvement from
variation caused by finite samples. We therefore use multi-seed averaging for the 1D expert and
paired, sample-level bootstrap resampling for model comparisons, all performed on the frozen
predictor without retraining, recalibration, or threshold adjustment. Three full-data 1D models
(seeds 42, 123, and 2025) are averaged before calibration; this ensemble is the final predictor,
with no seed selected according to Test1 or Test2 performance, and a 15-member fold-checkpoint
ensemble was examined but not adopted because it did not improve the frozen validation criteria.
     For review-oriented screening, a reject-option policy [40] was defined using the uncertainty-
ranking score given by the decision-boundary proximity di = 1 − |pi − τ |/ max(τ, 1 − τ ) with
τ = 0.47022256531284556. On training OOF this proximity provides an informative ranking:
as coverage decreases from 100% to 80%, accepted-sample error decreases and MCC increases
monotonically, whereas equal-count random rejection shows no comparable improvement; at the
prespecified 90% operating point the cutoff was frozen at d∗OOF = 0.47361765874159856 (Fig-
ure 6). Transferring this cutoff unchanged to Test1 and Test2 reduced the accepted-sample error
by about 2.2–2.5 percentage points and improved MCC and BACC, while equal-count random
rejection remained close to the full-coverage error (Table 8). The transfer uses no benchmark-
specific quantile, coverage, class prevalence, or label, so the naturally different realized cover-
ages reflect a single OOF-derived cutoff rather than retuned abstention rates. This supports
a review-oriented screening workflow in which boundary-proximal predictions are flagged for
review, although it does not constitute independent validation, and the proximity score is a
ranking statistic rather than a calibrated probability of prediction error. The original binary
prediction remains available for every abstained peptide.

3.6                          Limitations and Future Directions
      The most fundamental limitation stems from the single-output nature of three-dimensional
conformation prediction. Short peptides in solution typically populate multiple interconvert-
ing conformations, and toxicity is usually mediated by one particular conformation or class of
conformations; a single predicted structure cannot cover this multimodal distribution. This
is precisely why the project introduced multiple conformation generators (PepFlow and Al-
phaFold2/ColabFold, assigned by length range). However, different generators exhibit inherent
noise, and normalizing conformers across models and sources further amplifies this inconsistency:
activating the multi-conformer residual on the test sets produced negligible and inconsistent ef-
fects that did not justify a public inference protocol, and source-independent and fusion-aware
candidates also failed their prespecified retention gates (Section 3.3, Tables 5 and 6), so the con-
former residual was eventually disabled for benchmark and public inference and the structural
information-extraction capacity of the model was not fully released. On training OOF the resid-
ual improved MCC by only +0.00190 (p = 0.203, not significant), and expanding the conformer
cohort from 250 to 761 peptides did not pass the retention gates because the added records were
all negative. In addition, full-atom conformational sampling (e.g., PepFlow) is computationally
far more expensive than single-structure prediction, which limited large-scale generation and
deployment. We therefore hope that next-generation protein structure prediction models can
provide, at acceptable computational cost, multiple possible conformations and their probabil-
ities (for example, lightweight multi-conformer sampling based on flow matching or diffusion),
more closely matching the multimodal nature of peptide conformation and potentially further
releasing the value of conformational information in toxicity prediction.
      The second limitation is that the 1D sequence and 3D conformation dimensions are com-
bined with a fixed 0.72/0.28 weight rather than dynamically adjusted per sample. This may
partly stem from the first limitation: the inherent noise among different conformation predic-
tors masks the information that would indicate which dimension should be trusted for a given
sample, and when structural evidence is itself inconsistent across generators, the model cannot
reliably distinguish, per sample, the reliability of sequence evidence from that of conformational
evidence and therefore falls back to fixed weights. Consistent with this, sample-level confidence
gates and dynamic routing attempts did not pass the retention gates: the former produced

                                                                                  24
MCC values below the fixed default, and the latter improved ranking and calibration but did
not exceed the frozen default’s decision metrics (Section 3.4). This reflects that the two di-
mensions have not yet achieved sufficiently effective information complementarity; adaptive and
interpretable fusion based on evidence reliability, together with more consistent conformational
evidence sources, are directions for future work.


4    CONCLUSIONS
     GHXTox explores the complementary joint use of sequence and conformational evidence
by combining a task-adaptive LoRA-ProtT5–Bi-Mamba sequence expert with a pLDDT-aware
multi-scale structural expert through probability-level fusion; the two experts are independent
and their complementarity is characterized by the structural expert correcting a subset of se-
quence errors in a class-asymmetric way (Section 3.2). The structural expert integrates residue
geometry, full-atom-derived chemical sites, and sequence-conditioned structural evidence. A
separate multi-conformer residual was trained with the single-structure backbone frozen and is
retained only for controlled ablations. The final predictor disables that residual and uses the
structural expert’s single-structure base probability. The 1D/3D weights (0.72/0.28) and the de-
cision threshold (0.47022256531284556) were frozen from training OOF predictions and applied
in the current evaluation without benchmark-specific retuning; Test1 and Test2 are historical
benchmarks, not newly blinded external sets.
     On the frozen historical Test1 and Test2 benchmarks, the model reaches MCC values of
0.8705 and 0.6673 and AUPRC values of 0.9674 and 0.8046, and the final fusion improves AU-
ROC, AUPRC, and the Brier score relative to the calibrated 1D stream. The 3D expert correctly
classified 36.92% and 37.50% of the respective Test1 and Test2 errors made by the 1D expert,
with most of these structural rescues occurring among non-toxic peptides. An OOF-frozen se-
lective policy further reduced accepted-sample error to 2.71% and 3.16% at realized coverages
of 88.54% and 86.94%, respectively, without benchmark-specific cutoff selection. Conformer-
derived information produced only modest and non-uniform improvements, indicating that map-
ping conformational variation to toxicity-relevant evidence remains an important limitation. The
interpretability analyses describe model sensitivity rather than causal mechanisms.
     Several limitations remain, most notably the single-output nature of conformation predic-
tion and the fixed probability-level fusion between the two dimensions, as discussed in Section
3.6; both benchmarks were also observed during earlier model development, and the structures
are predicted rather than experimental. Looking ahead, we hope that more powerful three-
dimensional conformation models, capable of producing multiple conformations with their prob-
abilities at acceptable computational cost, together with dynamic, evidence-reliability-aware
fusion between the sequence and structural dimensions, will enable a deeper complementary
joint use of the two types of information, with experimental validation where feasible.
